How to review stage curtains and masking in auditorium renderings

A visual QA guide to stage curtain tracks, masking legs, borders, storage, sightlines and lighting in auditorium interior renderings.

Define the performance state before staging the camera

A theatre, school hall or multipurpose auditorium can have a front curtain, side masking legs, overhead borders and a backdrop, but not every venue uses every element. Begin with the stage plan, section, theatre consultant information and the intended use of the room. Name the scene: lecture, performance, rehearsal or empty-room handover. A dramatic image that mixes different operating states may communicate a space that cannot actually be arranged.

Separate approved geometry from theatrical placeholders

Ask for the latest stage layout, rigging or curtain schedule, track details, stage elevations, reflected ceiling plan and relevant lighting positions. Record revisions and identify which curtains, supports and finishes are confirmed. If no stage consultant has specified a system, use a visibly labelled schematic representation rather than inventing equipment capacity, fire ratings or operational clearances. A rendering communicates the agreed design; it does not approve the theatre machinery.

Check the front curtain opening and stack

Where a front curtain is proposed, establish whether it travels sideways, lifts or operates in another documented way. The closed image should show the intended overlap and bottom relationship to the stage. The open image must account for the fabric stacking or lifted position, not make the curtain vanish. Keep the proscenium opening and any adjacent wall treatment consistent across both images. A narrow front elevation is useful for catching false symmetry and an implausibly wide clear opening.

Model legs, borders and backdrops as distinct elements

Side masking legs conceal offstage views, overhead borders can mask equipment above the performance area, and a backdrop defines the rear visual field. Their spacing and heights depend on the documented stage design. Do not substitute one giant black plane for every layer: that can hide the actual depth of the stage, change perceived proportions and obscure planned doors or scenery access. Compare an audience-facing camera with a plan or side section that reveals the positions of each layer.

Follow track positions and support interfaces

A curtain track or flying position belongs to a specific line in the stage layout. Check its location relative to the proscenium, ceiling soffit, acoustic treatments, sprinklers, diffusers and luminaires. Where tracks or support brackets are meant to remain visible, keep them visible. Where concealed, follow the documented pocket or masking detail. Do not infer load-bearing capacity or safe suspension from the appearance of a rail in a CGI image.

Represent fabric fullness and finish without inventing performance

The apparent width of a curtain depends on its fullness, pleats, stacking and hanging condition. Use the selected product or a documented sample as the material reference; avoid a perfectly flat cloth surface if the specified curtain is pleated. Compare a neutral-lit crop with the final theatrical lighting. Dark fabrics still need readable folds and believable contact shadows. Do not imply a specific flame classification, acoustic absorption or blackout performance from texture alone.

Review audience views and side-stage exposure

A central hero camera can conceal exposed backstage zones visible from extreme side seats. Review representative audience positions, including the edges of the seating arrangement, against the current masking layout. Use a plan overlay to identify lines of sight that might reach the wings, backstage access or lighting positions. Any formal sightline or accessibility assessment belongs to the responsible specialists. The image should flag a possible issue, not certify that every seat works.

Coordinate curtains with the intended lighting scene

A brightly illuminated backdrop, black masking and an apparently sunlit audience area can be legitimate only when the chosen room state explains them. Agree whether house lights, stage lights, daylight openings and blackout treatments are active. Avoid placing a decorative spotlight beam through a solid masking curtain or adding theatrical fixtures without source information. The interior lighting review addresses the broader balance of natural and artificial light.

Keep projection and curtain states compatible

A retractable projection screen may share the visual field with front curtains, borders or backdrops. Show which element is in front and which is retracted for the chosen use. A screen that appears to pass through a curtain track is a coordination question, not a Photoshop problem. Compare the states with the auditorium projection screen guide, which focuses on AV geometry and audience visibility rather than stage textiles.

Prepare a concise review set

Use a frontal audience view for the curtain composition, a side section or annotated stage plan for the curtain lines, and a second image showing the alternate operating state. Label camera, curtain state, drawing revision and unresolved assumptions. Separate rendering errors from decisions needed from the architect, theatre consultant or supplier. Keep the approved geometry consistent before adding people, props or dramatic lighting.

Fictional example: a school performance hall

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. A hypothetical school hall uses a front curtain and two pairs of masking legs. The first image shows the front curtain open but removes its entire side stack, making the proscenium look wider than the coordinated plan. A review elevation identifies the missing stack. The revised image shows the documented parked position and flags an unresolved relationship between the second masking leg and a side door. No rigging capacity, fire performance or sightline compliance is claimed.

Final visual QA checklist and approval boundary

Confirm the room use and curtain state; latest stage plan and sections; front-curtain operation and stack; masking legs, borders and backdrop positions; visible track interfaces; fabric finish and fullness; audience-side views; lighting and projection states; and all unresolved design decisions. The resulting render can demonstrate consistency with supplied documents. It cannot certify rigging safety, fire compliance, acoustic performance, access, operation or technical stage design.

Resolve stage textile assumptions before final CGI

Share the stage layout, sections, curtain schedule and intended room states to keep visual corrections separate from technical design decisions.
