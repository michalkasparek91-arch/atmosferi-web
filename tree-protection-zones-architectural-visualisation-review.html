How to review tree protection zones in architectural visualisations

Retaining a tree in a rendering is easy. Showing construction access, levels and protection constraints without misleading the viewer takes a coordinated review.

Why a retained tree needs more than a convincing canopy

A retained tree is a design constraint, not just a visual asset. A polished planning image can preserve its crown while quietly placing a new path, excavation or site compound where protection measures are required. The image then implies a buildable arrangement that the design team has not checked. This guide focuses on how to review the representation of tree protection during design and construction phases. It is not an arboricultural method statement or an assessment of tree health.

Separate three questions before modelling: what is surveyed and retained, what protection or exclusion zone is documented, and what the image is intended to show. A finished-development hero image and a construction-stage logistics illustration answer different questions. Neither should imply that a tree is protected merely because it remains visible.

Request a coordinated source pack, not a green mood board

Start with the latest topographical survey and tree schedule, a tree constraints or protection plan where available, architectural and civil drawings, relevant arboricultural method statements, site access or logistics plans and revision dates. Ask the project team which documents govern the view and who is responsible for approving tree-related changes. The root protection area, protective fencing line and crown extent may not be identical.

If a tree protection plan has not been prepared, mark that input as missing. Do not calculate a protection radius from a guessed trunk diameter or import a generic national rule into a different jurisdiction. A visualisation artist should flag the information gap, not issue a substitute arboricultural design.

Distinguish trunk, crown, root area and fencing

Build four separate review layers: surveyed stem position, depicted crown spread, documented root protection area and proposed physical barriers. The crown can extend beyond a protection boundary, and the underground root environment cannot be inferred reliably from the silhouette. Check plan coordinates, site levels and the scale of each layer against fixed survey points.

In an atmospheric final image the protection area may not be visible as a line on the ground. That is acceptable when the image depicts completed works, but the source scene should still preserve the documented constraint. For a construction-phase illustration, fencing and restricted areas must follow the approved drawing rather than an aesthetically convenient perimeter.

Review excavation and access before placing machinery

Compare haul routes, temporary roads, crane positions, stockpiles, parking, trenching and scaffolding with the documented exclusion zones. An excavator drawn next to a retained trunk may imply access or excavation that has never been agreed. Check whether the selected camera hides an apparent clash behind planting or a vehicle.

The visualiser should record conflicts for the architect, arboriculturist and construction team to resolve. Do not move a barrier, route or tree to improve composition without a revised design source. For construction-phase storytelling, use distinct approved phases rather than mixing future completed landscaping with temporary works.

Do not smooth away grade changes around trees

New retaining edges, paving, raised planters and regraded lawns can alter the apparent ground condition around retained trees. Compare existing and proposed levels near the stem and documented protected area. A rendering that seamlessly extends a new terrace beneath a mature tree can conceal unresolved excavation or fill.

Model only the grading solution that has been approved for depiction. If the landscape and civil packages disagree, leave an explicit coordination issue. A textured lawn or mulch surface is not evidence that roots, drainage and soil conditions have been protected.

Make temporary protection legible when it matters

For a construction logistics view, verify the specified fence alignment, gate locations, continuity and relationship to access routes. Avoid generic decorative garden fencing if the source calls for temporary protective barriers. Do not depict a conveniently open gap where the protection plan shows a continuous boundary.

For a completed scheme, remove temporary fencing only if the image clearly represents the post-construction condition. Keep a separate labelled construction-phase image if the presentation needs to explain how retained vegetation is protected while works take place.

Use plan overlays to challenge attractive perspectives

A street-level camera can show a healthy crown but conceal the ground beneath it. Review the scene once from above with the protection plan overlaid, once from the principal project viewpoint, and once from the most constrained access direction. Keep camera IDs and drawing revisions in the approval record.

Where a tree is outside the frame but its protected area intersects the site, include it in the coordination model anyway. Cropping an issue out of the final image does not resolve it. For overall planting identity and growth assumptions, use the landscape accuracy guide; this article deals specifically with protection constraints and construction-stage depiction.

Label design status instead of inventing certainty

A tree shown as retained may still be subject to assessment, redesign or approval. Use captions that distinguish confirmed retention from a proposed retention strategy. Do not call a rendering an arboricultural compliance certificate, and do not imply that a tree will survive construction because the final image shows it in full leaf.

A useful review comment is specific: “Tree T04 retained in the current landscape proposal; protective fencing and construction access subject to arboricultural and contractor coordination.” Adapt wording to the actual documented status, not the preferred marketing message.

Fictional example: a courtyard extension beside a mature tree

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A small housing extension is visualised beside a mature retained tree. The first courtyard render shows the tree accurately but adds a new paved pedestrian route close to the trunk. The construction-stage illustration also places a materials pallet beside it. The project team has not supplied a protection plan, so neither placement is represented as approved.

The visualiser logs two open questions, removes the unsupported pallet from the construction illustration and marks the proposed route as provisional pending coordination. Once a current plan is supplied, the team updates the shared model and reviews the same cameras. The image does not claim that the tree protection design has been technically validated.

Keep the approval trail attached to each image

Record the tree identifier, source revision, view or camera ID, unresolved assumptions, change owner and image version. If an arboricultural drawing changes, recheck every view where the protection zone, grading or temporary works could be affected. A single final JPG without its source context is not a reliable coordination record.

Separate visual approval from specialist approval. The architect may approve that the image matches issued design information; an arboriculturist or other responsible professional must review technical protection measures within their remit. For broader model handover responsibilities, see the BIM/CAD handover checklist.

Pre-publication review

  • Retained trees and identifiers match the latest surveyed information.
  • Protection zones are taken from a supplied plan, never guessed from crown size.
  • Temporary barriers, access, stockpiles and excavation are consistent with the depicted phase.
  • New ground levels and paving do not conceal an unresolved tree conflict.
  • Camera crops do not substitute for a plan-based coordination review.
  • Captions distinguish proposed retention from approved protection measures.
  • Approvals record the document revision and responsible reviewer.
