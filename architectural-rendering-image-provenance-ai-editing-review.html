How to review image provenance and AI-assisted edits in architectural renderings

A source-led review method for renderings that combine 3D scenes, retouching, photography and generative edits.

Start with what the image must prove

Architectural renderings can combine a 3D scene, photographs, generated vegetation, retouched skies and people. These elements do not all carry the same evidential weight. A client reviewing a facade for planning needs confidence that the building geometry and surroundings match the approved design. A marketing team may reasonably prioritise atmosphere, but must still avoid presenting speculative features as contractual facts. Write down the image’s audience and permitted use before editing starts. The same attractive composite may be unsuitable as evidence of existing site conditions.

Keep a source register for every material component

Record the originating model and revision, camera identifier, photographic plates, supplied reference images, textures and significant post-production layers. For each source, capture who provided it, when, its permitted use and whether it represents measured information, a design decision or generic atmosphere. A source register need not catalogue every pixel. It should allow a reviewer to trace anything that changes the meaning of the proposal: facade openings, site boundaries, planting, people, vehicles, signage and the visible neighbourhood. Do not assume that an image found online is licensed for commercial use.

Separate presentation edits from design edits

Classify edits by consequence. Adjusting contrast, removing compression artefacts and balancing colour are presentation changes. Replacing a sky or adding a person can change the perceived setting and use. Moving a window, widening a pavement, inventing a roof terrace or hiding an adjacent building changes represented design or context. The latter requires explicit design approval and an updated source reference, regardless of whether the change was made with a brush, generative fill or a 3D model. An AI tool is not an authority on the project.

Review generative changes at architectural detail level

Generative tools may produce convincing but false mullions, railings, door swings, paving joints, structural members, drainage details and shadows. Compare the final composite against the approved geometry using matched crops, not just a full-screen beauty review. Inspect repeated modules, reflections and interfaces where invented details can hide. Check people and vehicles for implausible scale or blocked accessible routes. Ask the editor to provide a before-and-after overlay for any local generative modification affecting the building or its immediate site.

Protect the integrity of photographic context

If a real photograph is the base, preserve relevant buildings, landforms, roads, trees and sightlines unless a documented proposal changes them. Label reconstructed or simplified context. A more dramatic sky, season or lens treatment can change perceived height, overshadowing and visual impact even when the architecture itself is unchanged. For viewpoint-dependent submissions, document camera position and field of view and follow the photomontage camera-matching checklist. Do not describe a generated background as a site photograph.

Check permissions without inventing ownership

A commissioned render does not automatically transfer every licence in its components. Confirm the contract covers the intended channels, territories, duration and derivative edits. Keep evidence for client-provided photography, third-party assets and any people who require appropriate releases. AI platform terms vary and may change; review the applicable terms rather than asserting blanket ownership or exclusivity. If a rights question remains unresolved, replace the asset with an authorised alternative before publication.

Introduce a two-gate approval workflow

Use one gate for architectural accuracy and a separate gate for publication readiness. The architect or design lead signs off geometry, materials, landscape and context against named revisions. The publishing owner then confirms captions, intended channels, licensing and the current export version. An approved design view should not be silently modified later to improve its appearance. If a material edit occurs after sign-off, return the affected crop to the design lead. This keeps aesthetic decisions from masquerading as design authorisation.

Make an edit log small enough to maintain

For each substantive edit, capture image ID, base render version, edit category, affected region, source or prompt reference where appropriate, reviewer, decision and date. Store a flattened final file plus an editable layered master when included in the agreed scope. An audit trail should identify which export was published and where, not merely which file was most recently saved. For the broader delivery package, use the rendering handover checklist.

Fictional example: a courtyard marketing view

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A development team approves a courtyard model with a planted edge and a narrow pedestrian route. During AI-assisted retouching, a generated tree obscures a ground-floor entrance and a new bench appears on the accessible route. The editor flags both regions, removes the bench and restores the entrance from the approved base render. The team keeps the atmospheric sky replacement but records it as illustrative. The published caption describes the view as an artist’s impression, not as a measured photograph.

Use a decision matrix for disputed edits

A practical review can use four decisions: accept as presentation-only, accept after architectural verification, revise against source, or remove pending rights confirmation. For example, colour grading may pass the first category; a revised balcony balustrade requires drawing verification; an invented adjacent facade should be revised; and an unlicensed photograph should not be published. Record the decision beside the crop so the same issue is not reopened by another reviewer. Avoid vague comments such as “make it more realistic” when the underlying question is factual accuracy.

Write captions that match the evidence

Use plain language about what the viewer is seeing. “Architectural visualisation of the proposed design” is more useful than an unqualified claim of photographic accuracy. If vegetation maturity, furniture, surrounding development or finishing details are illustrative, disclose that where material to the decision. Avoid burying all qualifications in a distant legal page. A caption cannot repair a fundamentally misleading image; it is a final clarity check after the content itself has been corrected.

Final release checklist

Before release, verify the correct design revision and camera; confirm no AI edit altered approved geometry without sign-off; inspect entrances, thresholds, railings, windows and boundaries at full resolution; reconcile photographic context; confirm third-party asset permissions; record meaningful edits; approve captions and channels; archive the exact published export; and assign an owner for later updates. If design changes after publication, follow the rendering change-control process rather than quietly replacing files without a record.
