How to publish confidential architecture projects without breaking an embargo

A practical release checklist for architecture studios handling unannounced commissions, private homes and client-approved press launches. The challenge is not just what the website says, but what its files, previews and metadata reveal.

By Michal Kašpárek, architect and founder of Atmosferi10 November 2026

Architecture portfolios often move between private review, client approval and public announcement. That transition needs an explicit release process. This guide is about confidentiality, embargo timing and unintended disclosure; it is not a substitute for legal advice or a general image-licensing checklist.

Decide whether the project can be public at all

Before selecting a hero image, ask whether the studio is permitted to identify the commission. A contract, competition rule, client instruction or press agreement may limit what can be published and when. Do not interpret possession of drawings as permission to put them online. Create a simple decision record: project identifier, proposed release date and time zone, approved public name, permitted claims, permitted assets, named approver and evidence of approval.

There are three useful outcomes: public now, public after a specified condition, or not approved for publication. A fourth state, “needs clarification”, must not silently become public because the website deadline arrives. If a clause is unclear, obtain clarification from the relevant parties rather than relying on this editorial checklist as legal advice.

Approve the precise publication scope

Permission to show a project at a private interview is not necessarily permission to publish it on a searchable website. Record whether approval covers the project page, homepage teaser, social cards, downloadable press pack, video, translated pages and third-party distribution. Include a version or asset list. An approval for a cropped facade photograph does not automatically extend to an uncropped plan or an animation that reveals additional spaces.

Keep design attribution and image licensing as separate checks. The portfolio credits and approval workflow explains who should be credited; this guide addresses the release boundary and the risk of revealing information too early.

Review the building's location and occupants

Private homes, sensitive workplaces and occupied buildings deserve a deliberate privacy review. A project title, street address, geotag, floor plan and window view can become identifying when combined. Consider using a broader location such as a city or region, but do not invent a false location. Remove names, security equipment details, room labels, personal possessions, licence plates and identifying reflections where they are not needed for the architectural story.

Ask whether the imagery reveals access routes, private gardens, alarm positions or views into neighbouring homes. This is not a blanket rule to conceal all architecture. It is a test of whether the proposed disclosure is necessary, accurate and approved. Anonymisation must not be used to disguise a material design change or misrepresent a planning submission.

Inspect the files, not just the visible pixels

Image exports may contain EXIF GPS coordinates, author names, timestamps or source filenames. PDFs can expose comments, layers, hidden text and document properties. A screenshot of a drawing can still include a title block or a readable address. Check the actual downloadable and embedded files with a metadata viewer and a visual inspection at full resolution.

For each asset, record the approved public filename and whether metadata removal was needed. Keep original files securely in the studio archive rather than stripping the only authoritative copy. Confirm that image descriptions and captions do not accidentally restore information deliberately omitted from the visual.

Treat staging and previews as potential disclosures

A guessed preview URL, publicly accessible staging site or shared screenshot can leak the project before launch. A noindex directive is a request to search engines, not access control. Likewise, robots.txt does not make confidential content private. Keep unreleased content behind genuine authentication or do not upload it to a public host.

Check open graph images, generated thumbnails, CMS API responses, sitemap entries, feeds and internal search results. A private project can be exposed by an automatically generated card even when its main page is hidden. Test from a logged-out browser, not only from the editor's session.

Coordinate the embargo with the people releasing the news

Write the release time with a time zone and name the person authorised to give the final go-ahead. Confirm whether the trigger is a fixed date, an award announcement, a planning decision or written client consent. For condition-based releases, do not schedule an unconditional publish job. Prepare the page as a private draft, then have a named editor confirm the condition before making it public.

Agree on who publishes the website, who releases press material and who handles social posts. If an announcement slips, every channel should remain unpublished. Publishing the website first and hoping nobody notices is a surprisingly unreliable embargo strategy.

Fictional example: an unannounced residential project

Fictional illustration, not an Atmosferi commission: A practice has permission to show two exterior renderings of an unannounced house after the client's public announcement. The approval excludes the precise address, floor plans and interior views. The website editor stores the page as an authenticated draft, prepares an approved regional location label, checks image metadata and disables social previews of the draft. On the announcement day, the editor confirms the client release before publishing the two authorised images.

If the announcement is postponed, the page stays private. The practice does not replace the address with a fabricated one, invent a completion date or describe the renderings as photographs of a built home.

Run a release acceptance test

After authorisation, verify the public page in a logged-out browser on desktop and mobile. Check that the title, captions, project status and credits match the approved version; no unauthorised files or location clues appear; social previews show the approved image; and every public link leads to the intended page. If the page should be indexable, verify its actual response, canonical URL, robots directive and sitemap inclusion. Indexing is a separate search-engine decision, not a launch guarantee.

Keep a dated release record with the approved page URL, asset versions, reviewer and any remaining restrictions. For general page structure, see preparing architecture projects for a website; for redesigns that change addresses, use the URL migration checklist.

Plan for a changed permission or a mistake

Approval can change, or an editor may discover an unapproved file after publication. Assign one contact who can request a rapid correction or takedown. Remove the exposed asset from public delivery, review cached previews and feeds, correct linked pages, and document what was changed. Do not assume deleting a CMS entry instantly removes copies from caches or external websites.

Where a page must be withdrawn entirely, choose an appropriate HTTP response and remove internal references and sitemap entries. Avoid redirecting a confidential project to an unrelated public project. If the disclosure has contractual, privacy or security consequences, escalate to the responsible professional advisers rather than treating it as only an SEO issue.

Final go/no-go checklist

Before publishing, confirm: permission for this exact page and its assets; timing and release condition; privacy of location and occupants; files inspected for hidden information; preview security before launch; public QA after release; and a named withdrawal owner. A missing approval is a no-go, not a task to fix after launch.
