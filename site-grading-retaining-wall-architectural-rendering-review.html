Field notes · Terrain and level coordination

How to review site grading and retaining walls in architectural renderings.

Sloping sites are easy to beautify and surprisingly easy to misrepresent. Review the evidence behind walls, steps and ground transitions before approving the image.

Why terrain errors survive beautiful renders

A persuasive exterior image can make a steep site appear almost level. The risk is not merely aesthetic: a missing retaining wall, concealed step or flattened driveway can misrepresent how a building meets the ground. Treat the terrain model as a design input, not a backdrop. This guide is for visual review and communication, not a substitute for civil design, structural checks or permission drawings. Establish whether the image is intended for internal coordination, planning discussion or marketing before approving it.

Collect the correct terrain evidence

Ask for the topographic survey, site boundary, architectural ground-floor plan, current landscape grading plan, civil levels and representative sections. Record the drawing issue, units and vertical datum. Survey contours may describe existing ground while the landscape plan shows proposed finished levels. Mixing the two can produce a convincing but impossible slope. Where information is missing, show a provisional terrain area and request a decision rather than inventing a smooth transition.

Build a level register before placing cameras

Create a compact register of existing spot heights, finished floor levels, door thresholds, pavement edges, driveway connection and retaining-wall tops and toes. Each number needs a source and datum. Confirm whether levels refer to structural or finished surfaces. A difference of only a few hundred millimetres can change the number of visible steps or the apparent height of a wall. Use a section to resolve conflicting plans before judging a perspective.

Show cuts, fills and boundary conditions

Compare existing and proposed terrain along the whole site edge, especially where a new garden sits above a neighbour or a driveway drops below the street. A rendering should not silently blend the grade across a property line. Identify cut slopes, fill areas, transitions to public pavement and any existing wall that remains. If the project team has not decided the boundary treatment, avoid presenting a seamless lawn as a finished solution.

Treat retaining walls as built elements

A retaining wall has a top, a toe, thickness, returns and visible junctions. Model its exposed height using coordinated levels, not a convenient eye-level estimate. The architectural team should confirm the intended finish, coping and any guardrail or fence shown. Structural stability, drainage behind the wall and foundations require specialist design; a rendered wall cannot demonstrate those outcomes. Do not hide a tall wall behind conveniently mature planting.

Check steps, ramps and driveways separately

A vehicle approach, accessible pedestrian route and garden stair have different design constraints. Trace each route in plan and section, including changes in gradient and landings. Make sure the visualisation preserves the actual number of steps and the relationship between door thresholds and adjacent paving. Avoid claiming accessibility from an attractive perspective. For entrance-specific questions, use the accessible-route review alongside the coordinated technical drawings.

Keep surface-water geometry visible

Falls, channels, swales and low points often disappear when the artist smooths the terrain for a clean composition. Ask the civil or landscape designer which features must remain visible and which are still provisional. Do not infer drainage performance from a dry sunny image. The flood-resilience guide covers scenario communication; this checklist focuses on whether everyday ground geometry matches the proposed design.

Choose cameras that expose level changes

Review one eye-level approach from the street, one view along the steepest crossfall and one oblique view showing the building-to-ground junction. A high camera can conceal a wall toe while a very wide lens can make a short slope look gentle. Keep camera height, focal length and target consistent when comparing revisions. Pair critical views with a labelled section; perspective alone is not reliable evidence of gradient or finished levels.

Fictional example: a sloping courtyard

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A concept residential building is set into a hillside. The first rendering shows an uninterrupted lawn between the terrace and neighbouring boundary. The proposed grading section instead indicates a level change requiring a retaining element and a short stair. The architect asks for the wall to be shown and for the lawn edge to follow the grading plan. The team does not invent wall drainage details or assert structural approval.

Make unresolved levels explicit

Supply the camera list, a terrain-model version, source drawing revisions and a small log of unresolved grade junctions. Mark assumptions such as unverified neighbouring levels, provisional wall heights or landscape details awaiting civil coordination. Marketing images should not quietly convert those assumptions into apparent facts. If a design change affects the terrain, recheck every view that includes the affected boundary or entrance.

Site grading visualisation sign-off checklist

Confirm that existing and proposed ground are not confused; all heights use one datum; thresholds and paving match sections; cuts and fills remain visible; retaining-wall tops and toes are credible; steps and driveways follow the design; drainage features are not erased; neighbouring ground is not fabricated; cameras reveal rather than conceal critical transitions; and unresolved assumptions are documented. The rendering is ready to communicate only what the supplied design evidence supports.
