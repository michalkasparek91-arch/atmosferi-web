Architecture portfolio credits: a project attribution and approval workflow

A precise way to distinguish design authorship, collaboration, media rights and publication sign-off before an architecture project page goes live.

By Michal Kašpárek, architect and founder of Atmosferi8 November 2026

A strong architecture portfolio needs more than striking images. It must tell the truth about who designed the work, who helped deliver it, and who may authorise the photographs and renderings used to describe it. The workflow below helps studios review those claims before they become permanent public pages.

Why a credits list is not enough

A project may have a lead architect, an executive architect, a competition team, a visualization studio and a photographer. Their contributions are not interchangeable. When a website reduces everyone to a single “by” line, it can misrepresent the work even when every name is spelled correctly. Start with the question a visitor is trying to answer: who designed what, at which stage, and who created the material being shown?

This guide focuses on attribution decisions and publication approval for project pages. For image selection, file formats and basic project facts, use the project preparation guide. It does not replace contracts or legal advice.

Build a project-level attribution record

Create one record per project before anyone writes the public copy. Include a stable project ID, working title, project status, design phases represented, participating organisations, named contributors where appropriate, the proposed public credit, the source of that wording, the person responsible for approval and the date reviewed.

Separate verified facts from claims awaiting confirmation. “Architect of record” or “lead designer” should never be inferred from the order of names in an old presentation. Store the record with the project, not in a designer’s private inbox.

Describe roles at the correct level of detail

Choose role labels that match the actual contribution: concept architect, design architect, local or executive architect, interior designer, landscape architect, structural consultant, visualisation artist or photographer. Some projects require phase-specific labels because the competition design and delivered building were produced by different teams.

Avoid treating a list of all consultants as proof of shared authorship. Conversely, do not erase a collaborator simply because the portfolio owner commissioned the website. If the correct public wording is disputed, hold the affected page for review.

Keep image rights separate from design credit

A practice's right to describe its design work does not automatically include permission to reproduce every photograph, rendering or drawing. Track each asset separately: creator, owner or licensor if known, source, intended website use, required credit wording, geographic or time limits if specified, and evidence of permission.

Do not assume that a photographer credit alone grants a licence. Likewise, permission to show a competition image in a private pitch does not necessarily permit permanent publication on a public portfolio. Where evidence is missing, substitute an authorised asset or delay that image.

Make project status and contribution visible together

A competition proposal, an unbuilt concept and a completed building may share the same visual language, but the website must not imply they share the same outcome. Put status close to the project introduction, not only in a hidden CMS field. If the practice worked on an early phase but not the completed building, explain that boundary in plain language.

Review captions independently from the hero title. A photograph of the finished building next to a competition-era description can unintentionally imply responsibility for later changes. Captions should identify whether the image depicts the proposed scheme, an approved design or the built result.

Use a sign-off matrix rather than one vague approval

Define four separate checks: factual project data, contribution and authorship wording, media permissions and final web presentation. A project principal may approve design facts while a photographer or rights holder confirms usage terms. A web editor should not silently treat approval of one category as approval of all four.

Record the exact page version, asset IDs, reviewer, decision and date. For a changed hero image or rewritten authorship sentence, reopen only the relevant approval instead of assuming the old sign-off still covers it.

Resolve conflicting credits before publishing

When two source documents disagree, identify their dates and purposes. A competition board may credit a joint team while a later press release names the delivery architect. Neither document alone explains every phase. Ask the project owner to reconcile the discrepancy with the relevant parties, then retain the agreed public wording and the supporting correspondence.

Do not solve a dispute by replacing specific roles with “all rights reserved” or by deleting names. If resolution is pending, publish a narrower, verified description or keep the project private. The website should not become an accidental adjudicator of professional authorship.

Fictional example: a competition design that changed hands

Imagine a fictional studio called North Court Practice. It developed a competition concept with a landscape collaborator; a separate delivery team later adapted the scheme. A commissioned visualisation artist produced the competition images, while a photographer documented the completed building. None of these fictional parties represents an Atmosferi client.

A responsible portfolio page could state “Competition concept: North Court Practice with landscape design by [fictional collaborator]; later delivery by a separate team” only if that wording is verified. The competition images and completed-building photographs need distinct creator credits and publication permissions. If the studio cannot verify the later phase, it should avoid presenting the completed building as its own delivered work.

Make the CMS enforce the decision

Store design roles as repeatable structured fields rather than a single rich-text paragraph. Keep media credits on each asset so they follow the image into galleries and social previews. Add a review state such as draft, awaiting factual approval, awaiting media permission, approved or published.

The editor should be able to see when a licence expires or when a project is still a proposal. The architecture portfolio CMS guide covers broader publishing choices; this workflow specifies the data and review gates needed for defensible credits.

Check the public page, not only the database

Before release, inspect the desktop and narrow mobile layouts. Verify that long collaborator names wrap without clipping, credits remain readable below cropped images, and captions are still associated with the correct photograph after a gallery reorder. Test keyboard navigation and make sure credit information is not available only on hover.

Review Open Graph previews and downloadable PDFs too: an image may be reused in a share card or brochure without its on-page caption. Confirm that those uses are covered by the permission record and that the preview does not misstate the project's status.

Treat post-publication changes as new decisions

A new photograph, project completion announcement, merger of practices or request to correct a contributor's role can make an approved page inaccurate. Log the change request, compare it with the approved record, identify affected pages and exports, and obtain the relevant sign-off before republishing.

Keep a brief correction history internally. For public factual errors, update the page promptly and ensure cached previews or downloadable materials are reviewed where practical. The URL migration guide is useful when project naming changes also affect links.

Publication acceptance checklist

Confirm that the project status is accurate; each architectural role is specific and supported; phases are distinguished; media usage is authorised; each visible asset has the required attribution; unresolved disputes are not disguised; the final text and image set have named approvers; mobile galleries preserve credits; and any downloadable or social version has been checked.

The outcome is not a legal certificate. It is a repeatable editorial record that makes a practice's public claims easier to verify and maintain.
