How to review living-wall facades in architectural renderings

A living wall can transform a facade image, but the render must distinguish actual planting systems and documented details from an imagined mature green surface.

Why living walls need their own visual review

A planted facade is not simply a green texture applied to a wall. Depending on the design, it may use climbing plants on cables or trellis, planted modules, planters at different levels or another specialist system. Each arrangement changes the building's depth, visible support, maintenance strategy and appearance over time. An attractive rendering can accidentally turn a proposed planting scheme into a promise of full coverage. This guide focuses on whether the image communicates the documented design honestly. It does not assess horticultural viability, structural capacity, fire performance or compliance.

Request a coordinated planting and facade brief

Collect the latest elevations, wall sections, landscape specification, planting schedule and any specialist living-wall drawings. Confirm which system is proposed, its location, approximate support depth and status of approval. Ask whether the plants are shown at installation, at an agreed establishment stage or as an aspirational mature condition. Note the revision and source of every assumption. If species, irrigation or supports remain undecided, mark the visual as provisional rather than choosing attractive details from a reference photograph.

Show the correct support system

A climber on stainless-steel cables should not look like a dense prefabricated panel. A modular living wall has visible edges, depth and sometimes breaks around openings; planters and troughs need credible positions. Compare support spacing, offsets and attachment locations with the issued facade details. Avoid painting foliage directly onto glazing, vents or movement joints. Where fixings or support dimensions are not supplied, ask for clarification rather than inventing a construction detail that appears approved.

Keep windows, doors and technical openings legible

Check planting boundaries around operable windows, doors, ventilation grilles, signs, lighting and access panels. Dense leaves can conceal a window's opening direction or make a required access zone disappear. Review the same facade in an elevation-like view and in the intended marketing camera. Where the planted surface meets a balcony or entrance canopy, check the actual termination and planting geometry. The facade ventilation grille review addresses those openings in more detail.

Represent irrigation and drainage without promising performance

Some living-wall systems depend on irrigation, drainage collection, nutrient supply or monitored maintenance. Other designs use ground-rooted climbers with different needs. The renderer should identify which arrangement is specified and whether any visible pipes, channels, planter bases or service cabinets belong in the image. Do not draw water dripping from every module for visual effect, or erase an approved drainage tray because it interrupts a clean facade. Hydraulic design, water demand and leak protection remain specialist responsibilities.

Do not hide maintenance access

Ask how planting will be inspected, pruned and replaced, and how irrigation components can be reached. The approved design may require access routes, removable panels or specialist equipment. A rendering need not display every maintenance operation, but it should not show impossible permanent coverage over known service points. Compare with the facade maintenance access guide when the planting affects cleaning or inspection of adjacent envelope elements.

Separate installation appearance from mature coverage

A newly installed climber and a fully established facade can look radically different. Agree on the target year or growth stage for each image and record it in the review notes. Do not present a speculative growth rate, survival rate or year-round density as a measured result. A mature visualisation can be useful to explain design intent, but it should be identified as an illustrative future condition. If the brief calls for a launch-day image, show a plausible initial planting state instead of a uniform carpet of leaves.

Control season, orientation and visual consistency

Check the intended season and whether the selected species is evergreen or deciduous, without assuming its behaviour from a generic asset library. Lighting, aspect and local climate can affect the appearance, but a still image cannot predict plant health. Keep the chosen growth stage consistent between street views, courtyard views and animations. When comparing summer and winter scenarios, label both and do not use seasonal foliage to hide a structural or service detail.

Keep facade materials visible where specified

Living walls can soften an elevation while obscuring important architectural information. Verify the proportion of exposed masonry, render, timber or metal against the planting zones in the drawings. The foliage should cast believable shadows and sit at a plausible distance from the substrate; it should not merge into cladding or float in front of window frames. For general planting colour and species representation, see the landscape planting accuracy guide.

Use three purposeful review views

Start with a near-orthographic elevation or facade crop to check planted extents and openings. Use an oblique close view to examine support depth, planters and junctions. Finally review the hero camera at the intended publication size, where a living wall may conceal an entrance or make a facade look more continuous than the design. Keep camera positions stable across revisions so the client can distinguish a genuine design change from a different composition.

Fictional example: a courtyard office facade

Fictional illustrative example, not a client project or a tested planting system. A proposed office courtyard has cables for climbing plants between two rows of windows. The first visual shows a seamless vertical garden from ground to roof. A coordination review reveals that the current drawings specify discrete cable bays, exposed wall between them, a planter at ground level and a service opening near the base. The artist corrects the extent and labels the dense foliage as a hypothetical mature condition. The architect and landscape specialist still need to confirm species, support, irrigation and access.

Create an approval record that survives revisions

For each image, log the facade drawing revision, planting system, growth stage, season, visible maintenance elements and outstanding questions. Separate visual modelling errors from unresolved design decisions. If the planting specification changes, check the hero image, brochure crops, animation frames and any website gallery using that facade. A signed-off render confirms only the agreed representation at that revision, not the long-term performance of the living wall.

Living-wall rendering checklist

  • Planting zones and system type match the latest facade and landscape documents.
  • Support depth, modules, planters and terminations are not invented as final details.
  • Windows, vents, doors, signage and service access remain correctly represented.
  • Irrigation and drainage elements are shown only where supported by the design.
  • Maintenance access and inspection points are not silently concealed.
  • Growth stage, season and species assumptions are recorded.
  • At least one view exposes support depth and facade junctions.
  • All published images use consistent planting assumptions.
  • No image is described as proof of plant survival, structural safety, fire performance or water tightness.

Where information is missing, an explicit question is more useful than an immaculate but invented green facade.
