Template or custom website for an architecture studio?

Choose the right foundation for your architecture portfolio: an established template, a tailored system or a bespoke build. Compare real project publishing, ownership, costs and long-term maintenance before commissioning a website.

By Michal Kašpárek · Architect and visualisation specialist25 November 2026
In this guide Start with the studio Compare three approaches Protect the visual identity Plan content editing Performance and access Scope and real costs Migration and ownership Final decision checklist

A template is a starting point, not a verdict on design quality.

When an architecture studio commissions a website, “template or custom?” sounds like a simple price decision. It is actually a question about what needs to remain consistent, what needs to be individual, and who will maintain the project archive. A carefully configured template can produce a clear portfolio. A custom website can also be needlessly elaborate, slow and difficult to update. Neither approach is automatically more professional.

Begin with the practice rather than the platform. Is the work mainly residential or does it span building types? How many genuinely different project stories need to be shown? Are drawings, films and photography central to the presentation? Will several team members edit content? Are multiple languages, recruitment pages or development projects part of the real publishing plan? Those answers determine the useful degree of customisation.

Many studios do not need a new interface pattern for every page. They need one confident visual identity, a well-edited project archive and a small set of repeatable page types. Others have unusual portfolios, large image sequences or interactive services that justify bespoke components. Judge the choice against actual editing tasks and the experience of visitors, not the number of animations in a proposal.

Compare template, tailored template and bespoke website on the same brief.

There are more useful options than a cheap ready-made theme and a completely original build. A middle route uses a solid starting system with custom art direction, project templates and carefully selected components. Compare approaches on exactly the same scope or the apparent price difference will be meaningless.

These are decision characteristics, not guaranteed outcomes. A poorly implemented bespoke site can be harder to change than an excellent template. A highly customised theme may cost nearly as much to maintain as a bespoke system. Ask every provider to demonstrate the exact editing and publishing experience they propose, not just a polished static mock-up.

Spend design effort where an architecture portfolio is genuinely distinctive.

Architectural identity lives in proportions, sequencing, typography, project curation and the treatment of photography. It does not necessarily require inventing a custom navigation control. A restrained template can be effective when project pages need a familiar rhythm; a bespoke design becomes useful when that rhythm obscures how the studio works or forces every project into the same generic grid.

Take a difficult portfolio example: one project needs a full-screen exterior image, another needs a detailed sequence of plans, and a third is a renovation with comparable before-and-after photography. If the chosen system can represent each story coherently, the visual foundation may be sufficient. If every exception demands fragile workarounds, the template is probably the wrong starting point.

Review the homepage and at least two real project pages before committing to an approach. Use actual approved images, short project facts and a long heading rather than placeholder content. A beautiful demonstration using only one square image tells you little about a 25-image case study with tall drawings and captions. The architecture studio homepage guide explains how to turn the first screen into a useful introduction; the project case-study template covers the deeper editorial structure.

Do not treat motion as proof of custom craftsmanship. If a transition improves hierarchy or explains the architecture, use it; if it blocks text, breaks mobile interaction or makes the project harder to browse, leave it out. A portfolio should foreground the work, not demonstrate how many effects its builder knows.

The content model matters more than the logo on the CMS.

The strongest argument for a custom or tailored website may be invisible to the visitor: the ability to add a project without asking a developer to rebuild the gallery. Before choosing a system, simulate normal studio work. Can an editor create a project, arrange images, add drawings and captions, update a project status, publish a second language and then correct a credit?

At minimum, a useful architecture project record should separate title, type, location where publishable, year or status, narrative, images, captions, collaborators and the studio's actual role. More complex studios might need multiple photographers, embargo rules, languages, competition statuses or site-level development data. Storing everything inside one unstructured rich-text field may look easy on day one and become painful after fifty projects.

A template with good structured content may be better than a custom front end that still relies on manual HTML editing. Conversely, bespoke content modelling may be justified where the practice has a large archive and recurring, nonstandard project formats. Use the architecture portfolio CMS guide to compare static, traditional and headless editing patterns based on actual work rather than product fashion.

Ask who owns the content after handover. Can the studio export project titles, image references and text? Who holds hosting, domain and administrator access? Which changes require a developer? Ownership and repeatable editing procedures should be explicit whether the visual layer began with a template or with a blank file.

Neither route guarantees speed, accessibility or search visibility.

A template may load unnecessary scripts because its demo includes features your studio never uses. A custom site may also be slow because it downloads every full-resolution render and starts an expensive video immediately. The implementation, hosting and content workflow decide performance. Measure the pages that matter, especially the homepage hero and image-heavy case studies.

Ask providers to demonstrate sensible image sizes, lazy loading below the fold, a promptly visible main image and stable gallery proportions. Test mobile menus, keyboard access, focus states, contrast and reduced-motion preferences. The Core Web Vitals checklist offers a practical performance review for architecture portfolios. For accessibility, the W3C WCAG 2.2 recommendation gives a more reliable reference than promises that a particular platform is automatically compliant.

Search visibility depends on much more than choosing a framework. Useful project descriptions, crawlable links, correct canonicals, a coherent language strategy and appropriate redirects matter on either route. If an agency promises rankings simply because it builds a custom site, ask what it will actually test and maintain. Good technical foundations help discovery, but they do not guarantee enquiries.

Compare the cost of a working website, not two hero screenshots.

A fair quote includes content preparation, page templates, CMS setup, image optimisation, responsive design, contact forms, technical SEO, launch checks, hosting decisions and handover. Templates often reduce early design and component development effort, but licensing, plugins, content migration and unsupported custom changes can add ongoing costs. Custom systems require clear documentation and someone who can maintain them.

Ask for a fixed deliverable scope and a separate list of exclusions. At the time of writing, Atmosferi publishes starting website prices for Portfolio, Practice and Launch packages. Those are its own indicative prices excluding VAT, not industry averages or a promise that every brief fits one tier. Films, further architectural visualisations, 360° tours and advanced integrations are quoted separately. Use the architecture website cost and scope guide to compare offers on like-for-like deliverables.

A seemingly inexpensive quote can become costly if every new project layout, language or content update requires a paid intervention. A costly proposal can also be unjustified if the studio needs only a straightforward portfolio and three or four regular page types. Evaluate the likely editing and support needs over several years, not just the invoice for the first launch.

Both are hypothetical briefs. Their purpose is to show why the right implementation depends on content and workflow, not a generic price-per-page formula.

Plan migration and ownership before selecting the platform.

A redesign does not start from a blank internet. Existing project pages may already have links from architecture publications, awards, partners and search results. List the old URLs and identify the new destination for each one before changing the site structure. Reuse stable URLs where practical and arrange permanent redirects when URLs genuinely need to change.

Google Search Central's site-move documentation explains the need for redirects, updated links and careful verification when addresses change. A design change is not an excuse to discard a functioning archive. The architecture portfolio URL migration guide provides a project-by-project checklist.

During handover, record where the source code, CMS data, media originals, domains, analytics and deployment credentials belong. Test what happens when the original supplier is unavailable. Can the owner access the system and move it if needed? Even a bespoke site should be transferable; even a template-based site needs documented licences, integrations and update responsibilities.

Use a short decision brief before requesting proposals.

Try answering these questions in writing: Who is the primary audience? What must the first visit communicate? How many projects exist, and how many types of project page are needed? Who edits content and how often? Which languages will actually be maintained? What are the must-have interactions? Which current URLs should survive? What can be postponed until a second phase?

If the answers describe a small portfolio with conventional case studies and light updates, a well-chosen template or tailored foundation may be sufficient. If they describe complex project types, many editors, evolving services or specialised interactions, investigate a custom content model and components. This is an evaluation framework, not a hard threshold based on project count.

  • The proposal explains why its chosen approach fits the real portfolio.
  • At least two actual projects have been tested in the proposed design.
  • Website ownership, code access, CMS access and hosting responsibilities are clear.
  • The editor can add a project without accidental layout damage.
  • Special page types, multilingual editing and image rights are included or excluded explicitly.
  • Mobile, accessibility, search and performance QA are part of the scope.
  • Existing pages have a documented URL and redirect plan.
  • Licensing, support and recurring costs are disclosed separately.
  • Visualisations, films, 360° tours and advanced integrations have their own scope.
  • The quote includes a realistic review, approval and handover process.

Send the same written brief to competing providers and ask each to demonstrate the proposed editing workflow. The architecture website project brief template gives a starting structure. The strongest answer is not always “fully custom”: it is the smallest dependable system that presents the practice properly and remains easy to run.
