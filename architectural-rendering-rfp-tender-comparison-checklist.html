How to write an architectural rendering RFP and compare proposals

A fair rendering tender compares scope, assumptions and deliverables, not just the cheapest headline number.

Define the decision before the image count

A rendering request for proposals should start with the intended decision. Planning submissions, design competitions, investor presentations and apartment marketing require different levels of context, fidelity and supporting evidence. Explain the audience, design stage, approval authority and deadline. An attractive image is not necessarily a technical verification view. If the brief simply requests several high-quality images, bidders may price different work without making the difference visible.

Give every bidder the same input register

List drawings, BIM/CAD models, site surveys, finish schedules, landscape plans and reference photographs, with version dates and status. Distinguish approved information from provisional assumptions. A supplied model may need substantial clean-up before it is render-ready. Ask bidders to identify missing data rather than inventing design details. The BIM/CAD handover guide explains how to prepare geometry and material decisions.

Number each view and specify its purpose

Create a camera schedule with IDs, viewpoint descriptions, interior or exterior location, required context, day or night condition and intended output. Mark camera positions as fixed or subject to approval. A wide aerial view can require a much larger context model than an eye-level courtyard image. If the number of views may change, ask for a transparent option price rather than an undefined allowance. Give every bidder the same camera list.

Separate modelling from image production

Identify who supplies the building geometry, who models surrounding streets and landscape, and whether furniture, people, vehicles, planting and post-production are included. Clarify how the team handles unresolved architectural details and whether material selection is approved or illustrative. Animation, interactive models and virtual tours need separate specifications. Otherwise a quote for two still images can quietly be compared with a quote that includes an entirely different production package.

Specify review gates and change control

A practical sequence is input acceptance, camera/massing preview, material-and-light preview, detail review and final export. Assign one person to consolidate comments. Define whether changes to approved architecture, extra camera angles and new design alternatives are treated as additional work. A promise of unlimited corrections is hard to compare unless the scope of a correction is defined. See the rendering feedback guide for a structured review process.

Name the files and usage rights

Specify the required pixel dimensions, colour space and final image formats for web and print. Do not assume that a JPEG includes layered Photoshop files or an editable 3D scene. Ask what rights are granted for brochures, social media, planning submissions and future campaigns. Third-party people cut-outs, photographs and 3D assets may have redistribution limits. For a detailed delivery plan, see the source-file handover checklist.

Include client review time in the programme

Ask for dates for input freeze, first preview, comments, revisions and final export. Separate the studio's production time from the architect's approval window. An unresolved façade or changing apartment layout may affect the critical path more than rendering speed. Require bidders to state dependencies and how they will handle late information. A credible schedule identifies who must decide what, and by when.

Compare proposals with one response matrix

Require each proposal to answer the same headings: view IDs, modelling scope, context, visual assets, approval gates, delivery formats, licences, programme, exclusions, assumptions and optional extras. Price alone is not a fair comparison when one bidder includes context modelling and another excludes it. Ask clarification questions before ranking bids, and circulate material clarifications equally to every invited studio.

Evaluate relevant work, not just polished imagery

Look for examples matching the project's scale and setting. Ask which parts of a reference image were created by the bidding team, and distinguish commissioned projects from fictional concepts. Check whether geometry, material appearance and camera framing support the design rather than simply creating drama. Do not request confidential client files or assume that reference imagery can be reused in your own marketing.

Fictional example: three courtyard proposals

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi client project. An architect requests two courtyard views. Bid A assumes the supplied BIM model needs no work and includes two web JPEGs. Bid B includes neighbouring buildings, camera studies and print masters. Bid C offers one still plus a frame from an animation. The headline figures cannot be compared until the architect clarifies the context boundary, the number of independent cameras and required outputs. The example illustrates a scope problem, not a real bid or price result.

A reusable RFP structure

  1. Project objective, audience and decision date.
  2. Input register with revision and approval status.
  3. Numbered view schedule and image purposes.
  4. Modelling, context and post-production responsibilities.
  5. Preview gates, feedback owner and change rules.
  6. Output formats, usage permissions and source-file options.
  7. Schedule, dependencies, exclusions and optional services.

Keep one authoritative version of the brief. If a bidder identifies a missing requirement, share the clarification with every invited team before comparing offers.

Tender comparison checklist

  • Every bidder priced the same numbered camera schedule.
  • Model quality and missing inputs are declared.
  • Context, planting and entourage are explicitly included or excluded.
  • Review stages and design changes are described.
  • Output dimensions, file formats and intended uses are specified.
  • Usage rights and source-file expectations are clear.
  • Client review time is included in the schedule.
  • Optional costs and exclusions are visible beside the quote.
  • Reference work is relevant and fictional concepts are labelled.
  • The selected scope will be attached to the commission.
