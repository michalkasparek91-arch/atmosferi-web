How to visualise phased developments without misleading buyers or residents.

The completed masterplan and the first occupied phase are not the same place. This checklist helps teams communicate what will exist at each milestone, what remains temporary and what is still proposed.

Define the question for each phase

A phased masterplan rarely has one truthful image. A sales team may want to show the eventual completed neighbourhood, while a resident needs to know what the first occupied building will face for the next two years. Those are different questions and deserve different images. Before commissioning visuals, write down the intended audience, phase date, decisions being communicated and what is not yet fixed. Separate the completed vision from an interim-condition view. A rendering of the final masterplan should not quietly stand in for the environment available on first occupation.

Anchor every image to a dated programme

Ask the design and delivery teams for a dated phase schedule, site boundary plan and construction access strategy. Use phase IDs rather than vague labels such as “soon” or “later”. Record what is built, under construction, retained, removed and proposed at each milestone. If the programme is preliminary, state that the sequence is indicative. Renderers should not invent handover dates or assume simultaneous completion simply because all buildings exist in a BIM model.

Make a phase-state matrix before modelling

For each building, road, public space and landscape element, assign one of five states: existing and retained; completed and accessible; active construction; temporary condition; or future proposal. The matrix is more useful than a layer named “phase 2” because a road might be open while its adjacent planting is not established. Confirm the state of pedestrian connections, parking, fire access and shared amenities with the responsible design team. Do not interpret a visual as proof of regulatory compliance.

Use matched viewpoints to reveal changes

Choose a small set of stable camera positions at pedestrian height, plus an overview if it clarifies the overall plan. Keep location, lens, crop and horizon consistent when showing the same view at different dates. A dramatic aerial of the final phase and a ground-level photograph of the first phase cannot fairly explain the transition. If a camera position will be inside a construction zone in an earlier phase, say so and add a nearby accessible viewpoint. Our controlled comparison guide explains why matched presentation settings matter.

Depict temporary works without inventing detail

An interim phase may include fencing, hoarding, temporary lighting, service routes, storage and incomplete edges. These details affect how the place feels and functions. Obtain a reviewed site-logistics drawing rather than guessing a fence line. Where a contractor has not finalised the temporary works, use a clearly marked diagrammatic zone or annotation, not a photorealistic arrangement that looks approved. A construction sequence image is a communication aid, not a construction method statement.

Show planting maturity and inaccessible spaces

New planting does not instantly become the mature landscape in a final marketing view. For interim views, distinguish newly planted trees, existing trees and future landscape areas. Check whether the park, courtyard or play space will actually be open to residents at the illustrated milestone. Avoid filling unfinished plots with mature trees merely to soften the image. See the landscape accuracy checklist for evidence and maturity assumptions.

Represent occupied neighbours and site interfaces

A first-phase building may be occupied while adjacent plots are active sites. Show the relevant massing, screening and circulation honestly. The future building can be represented with a neutral mass if its design is not fixed; do not imply a finished facade. Review privacy, outlook and daylight claims separately with the relevant technical information. A beautiful image cannot establish that noise, access or construction disturbance has been resolved.

Use labels that survive being shared out of context

Every phase visual should identify the phase or milestone, issue date, model revision, viewpoint and whether it is an illustrative proposal. A small caption in the webpage body is insufficient if the image will circulate in a sales PDF or social post without that page. Put the essential status on the exported asset or in a tightly coupled caption. Avoid definitive wording such as “available at handover” unless the project team has verified it.

Fictional example: a three-stage courtyard

Fictional example. A development consists of three residential buildings around a shared courtyard. Building A opens first, B is under construction and C is still a proposal. The final-vision render shows all three buildings and mature planting, explicitly labelled “illustrative completed masterplan”. The first-occupation render uses the same camera but shows A occupied, the boundary to B's site and the temporary pedestrian route. The team marks C as a neutral future mass, not a finished design. A separate annotated plan identifies which courtyard areas remain closed. The example is invented and makes no claim about actual delivery dates, customers or results.

Approve evidence, not just aesthetics

A practical sign-off involves the architect for design status, the development manager for phase boundaries and programme, the landscape designer for planting, and the communications owner for captions. Record each approval against an asset ID and issue date. A visually appealing image may still be inaccurate if it shows a completed access route too early. If the programme changes, treat affected images as controlled revisions. The rendering change-control guide covers updates after launch.

Package each phase as a traceable set

Deliver named images, a matching phase-state matrix, an assumptions log and a short legend. Keep the source model revision and approved camera IDs with the files. Make a separate folder for final-vision images so they cannot accidentally replace first-occupation visuals. When handing content to a web team, specify where each image may be used and which captions must accompany it. For a broader website handover, see the development website content checklist.

Pre-publication checklist

Confirm that every image has a phase ID and dated source, that completed and temporary elements are distinguished, that public access and planting match the reviewed milestone, and that future buildings are not presented as approved or built. Check the legend in a phone-sized crop and in a downloaded image. Finally, ask a reviewer unfamiliar with the project what they think will exist at first occupation. If the answer differs from the approved phase plan, revise the communication before publication.

Keep the visual story aligned with the programme.
