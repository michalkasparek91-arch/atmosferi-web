How to review brick bond and coursing in architectural renderings

A practical review workflow for brick bonds, course heights, openings, corners and mortar joints in architectural visualisations.

Why brick pattern is a design decision

A brick façade can look plausible in a distant image while communicating the wrong bond, course count or alignment at openings. Repeating a generic texture is not equivalent to modelling the specified masonry. This guide focuses on the visual representation of documented design intent, not structural masonry design, construction tolerances or building-code approval. Start by identifying whether the scene is a concept study, coordinated design or a built-condition record. Never imply that a proposed arrangement is approved merely because the rendering is photorealistic.

Collect the right drawings before opening the render

Request current elevations, wall sections, brick format and mortar-joint assumptions, setting-out dimensions, corner and reveal details, and any sample-panel photographs that the team has permission to use. Record revision dates and whether the bond is specified or illustrative. A manufacturer texture alone rarely resolves how the masonry turns a corner or meets a window. When the drawings do not show a decision, mark it unresolved rather than inventing a convincing pattern.

Check the vertical module and cumulative drift

Compare the apparent number of courses between known datums such as a sill, lintel and floor level. The relevant module combines brick height and bed-joint thickness; a texture scaled by eye can drift significantly over a full storey. Check that a brick course does not magically compress to fit the last centimetres below a parapet. In a perspective image, verify course count against an orthographic elevation or model measurement, not against pixel spacing alone. Any precise dimensions must come from the project's issued documentation.

Distinguish bond pattern from random texture variation

Running bond, stack bond, Flemish bond and other arrangements create different visual rhythms. Identify the actual specified pattern and verify the offset or header distribution where visible. Randomised brick colours may add realism, but they cannot fix an incorrect bond. At a close camera, check that the pattern repeats consistently across adjacent façade planes without obvious UV seams. A decorative texture should not accidentally suggest structural headers or a cavity-wall construction method that has not been designed.

Inspect corners, piers and returns in three dimensions

The easiest place to spot a flat texture pretending to be masonry is a building corner. Compare the proposed corner arrangement with the architectural detail: does the bond wrap, terminate against a trim, or change at a movement joint? Inspect narrow piers between windows and brickwork around deep reveals. Avoid visually impossible slivers introduced by automatic texture mapping. Where the designer has not resolved cuts and closures, do not substitute a fabricated technical solution in the final image.

Review sills, lintels and special brick details

Window heads, arches, soldier courses, recessed bands and projecting brickwork are frequently lost when the whole façade uses one material map. Check the drawings for deliberate changes in orientation, depth or bond. At door and window openings, inspect the relationship between course datums and frame positions. A dark shadow under a sill may hide a mismatch; temporarily review the image under flatter light. If a feature is only a conceptual placeholder, label it as such in the review notes.

Keep mortar colour, thickness and recess credible

Mortar joints influence the façade's overall tone and perceived scale. A very dark, oversized grid can make fine brickwork resemble tiles; a jointless texture can make the wall read as a printed surface. Match the specified joint treatment where available, including bed versus perp joints, recess and colour. Do not use a rendering to certify mortar performance, weather resistance or workmanship. Separate the visual finish decision from specialist technical approval.

Use both orthographic and eye-level checks

Review one flat elevation or orthographic crop for module, bond and continuity, then inspect the final eye-level marketing camera for the perceptual result. Grazing sunlight can exaggerate relief, while overcast light reveals whether brick geometry exists beyond a bump map. Compare a close-up around an opening and a full façade view. Keep the same camera IDs across revisions so that a revised bond is not accidentally approved in one view and left outdated in another.

Log discrepancies as design or rendering issues

A useful review note identifies façade zone, camera ID, drawing revision, issue type, proposed correction and decision owner. Separate ‘render texture mis-scaled’ from ‘bond detail not supplied’. The first can be corrected by the visualisation team; the second requires an architect or façade specialist decision. Freeze the source revision used for final export, and recheck all linked views when the masonry setting-out changes.

Fictional example: a brick apartment entrance

Fictional illustrative scenario, not an Atmosferi commission or a construction-approved detail. A residential entrance is shown with a running-bond brick façade. The hero image looks convincing, but the brick courses do not align with the specified sill datum, and the texture resets abruptly at a recessed doorway. The architect supplies the latest elevation and confirms which entrance reveal details remain provisional. The artist corrects the vertical module and UV mapping, then provides a flat-light comparison crop. The unresolved reveal closure stays documented rather than being silently invented.

Keep rendering approval separate from buildability

An architect can approve that the image represents the intended brick rhythm and material appearance. The engineer, contractor and relevant specialists remain responsible for structural behaviour, movement, moisture management, fire safety, tolerances and buildability. Do not describe a visualisation as proof that the masonry can be executed as shown. Link the approved image to its design revision and preserve the open-issue register.

Final pre-publication checklist

Before publishing, confirm the documented bond; brick and mortar module; course datums at openings; corner and reveal continuity; special soldier or recessed courses; joint colour and depth; scale in both close and distant views; and consistency across every approved camera. Confirm that provisional details are disclosed, and retain the drawing references used to make the comparison. For adjacent decisions, use the movement-joint guide and material-finish approval checklist.

Coordinate the masonry before final export

Share the latest elevations, brick specification and intended camera views to establish what the rendering should show and what remains unresolved.
