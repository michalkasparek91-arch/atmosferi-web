Before and after renovation photography: a portfolio guide for architects.

A clear, accurate renovation story starts long before the finished photographs. Learn how to pair existing and completed views, describe real design changes, handle interactive comparisons and publish with appropriate permissions.

By Michal Kašpárek · Architect and visualisation specialist25 November 2026

Show the architectural change, not just a dramatic contrast.

Renovation and adaptive reuse projects often make more sense when a visitor can see the conditions the architect started with. A finished photograph may be beautiful, but it cannot on its own explain which spaces were retained, what was repaired or how the brief changed a difficult existing building. A thoughtful before-and-after sequence gives the project a clear beginning and a credible result.

That does not mean every existing building needs an exaggerated reveal. The real value may be subtle: daylight reaching a previously enclosed corridor, an accessible entrance that replaced a difficult threshold, or a repaired façade whose original character remains visible. An architect's website should explain what was changed and what was deliberately kept. The comparison supports that explanation; it is not a substitute for it.

Define the status of both images before publication. A genuine before / completed pair is different from an existing-condition photograph compared with a proposed visualisation. Both can be useful, but they communicate different things. Do not label an unbuilt render “after” without making its proposed nature explicit. Avoid claiming that a planning proposal has been constructed or that a completed photograph represents work undertaken by the wrong team.

Start the editorial brief with one sentence: “This comparison should help the reader understand how [verified design change] addressed [actual condition].” If the image pair does not show the stated change, choose a different viewpoint or write a more precise caption.

Plan matching camera positions before the renovation begins.

The most useful “before” photographs are often taken long before anyone thinks about the website. A survey visit, demolition inspection or first client meeting may be the only chance to record the existing space unobstructed. Make future comparison photography part of the project documentation brief instead of hoping that someone finds a suitable phone picture two years later.

Mark the viewpoint on a plan or in a site-photo register. Keep enough fixed architectural reference points in frame, such as a window opening, staircase landing or adjacent building, to reconstruct the position later. Record the height, approximate direction, focal length or camera field of view where available, and any site-access restrictions. For interiors, a fixed doorway or column may be a more reliable anchor than the edge of loose furniture.

Try to maintain a comparable camera position, orientation and framing between the existing and finished states. The AIA's project photography tips recommend anticipating before-and-after pairs from nearly the same position and direction. They do not require an artificial visual match where the architecture has changed so much that the former viewpoint no longer exists.

When a perfect match is impossible, explain the difference instead of pretending the cameras are identical. A new partition might remove the original sightline; a demolished wall can create a view that never existed before. An approximate side-by-side comparison with honest captions is better than a falsely precise slider alignment.

Example viewpoint record · fictional renovation
PAIR INT-03 — Former corridor / new shared room Purpose: Show how circulation and daylight changed Existing view: [photo ID, date, camera position] Completed view: [photo ID, date, recheck position] Reference points: north window / retained brick pier Field of view: [record, or mark unknown] Design status: completed / proposed [verify] Photographer and usage rights: [approve separately] Pair status: aligned / approximately aligned / different view

All fields above are illustrative placeholders, not records of a real commission.

Tell the story in the order the visitor can understand.

There is no need to publish every site-visit photograph. Start by showing the finished space clearly enough to interest the visitor, then reveal the existing condition where the comparison makes a specific design decision visible. For an explanatory case study, it may be better to begin with the original constraint, the architectural response and the completed space. Choose the sequence to serve the story rather than following a rigid marketing formula.

A practical renovation project page might open with one strong completion image and concise facts, then provide two or three before-and-after pairs. Follow these with a small drawing or annotated plan showing what was removed, retained or added, and end with a relevant material or construction detail. If the original condition was hazardous or unusually deteriorated, avoid implying that every member of the project team was responsible for it.

Give each pair a short, verifiable explanation. “A new glazed opening connects the former workshop with the courtyard” communicates more than “a stunning transformation.” A note about retained stonework or reused structure may be equally important because it shows restraint and judgment rather than sheer visual difference.

Keep architect, consultant, contractor and photographer contributions correctly credited. Use the architecture project case-study template to document the brief, the decision and the result instead of leaving the photographs to carry the whole story.

Use side-by-side images first; add a slider only when it helps.

A draggable before-and-after slider can reveal a transformation elegantly, especially when both photographs share a camera position and crop. It is not automatically the best layout. Sliders can hide half of the image, be awkward on touchscreens and make image information unavailable to visitors who cannot drag or use a mouse. A simple labelled side-by-side pair may be easier to scan and understand.

If the design calls for a slider, provide both photographs independently in the document as a usable fallback, or an accessible control that allows the full original and finished states to be examined. Label the two states visibly and in accessible text. Make the control usable from a keyboard and with touch, and test its focus behaviour at mobile widths. Avoid autoplay movement that repeatedly changes the image without the user's intention. The W3C WAI carousel guidance discusses control, keyboard access and avoiding unwanted automated motion; apply the same principles when choosing interactive comparisons.

Do not hide the explanation inside the image alone. Provide captions describing the difference and any unavoidable mismatch in view. If the before shot is badly exposed or lower resolution, it is fine to preserve it as documentary evidence rather than artificially polishing it to resemble a marketing photograph.

For very different viewpoints, use two distinct figures rather than attempting a slider that suggests geometric registration. If the client needs a precise, planning-grade comparison, that is a separate controlled photomontage requirement, not an ordinary renovation portfolio treatment. See the photomontage camera-matching checklist for those concerns.

Preserve visual honesty when preparing the images.

Basic exposure correction, white balance and lens adjustments can improve legibility, especially when existing-condition photographs come from a different camera. But edits must not change the architectural evidence. Avoid adding or removing windows, changing openings, hiding neighbouring structures or using generous cropping to imply that work was completed beyond the actual scope.

Do not make the before photograph arbitrarily dark and the after photograph artificially bright just to increase the emotional contrast. Daylight, weather and season may genuinely differ; explain those circumstances when they materially affect the comparison. Match reference points, not mood. If the original image is low quality, show the limitation rather than claiming a perfect technical match.

Architectural visualisations need their own labels. An existing-condition photo beside a proposed 3D image should say “Existing condition” and “Proposed design visualisation”, never silently substitute “Before” and “After” for finished construction. If a rendering incorporates AI-generated or edited elements, review provenance, geometry and approval under the image provenance and AI editing guide.

The architecture studio should approve the final pair, caption and status together. A web designer can prepare the comparison, but cannot independently certify which elements were built, retained or changed. The approval record should survive future website updates or gallery migrations.

Clear image permissions and client privacy before publishing.

Existing-condition photography can expose more private information than finished promotional photos: personal belongings, house numbers, security systems, identifiable people and details of occupied homes. Review every image at its published resolution, not just as a small CMS thumbnail. Crop or redact sensitive details where appropriate, and verify that the edited image still conveys the intended architectural change.

Check whether the homeowner or property owner has agreed to public publication and whether the photographer granted the required digital usage rights. These are separate questions. A right to use a photograph in a tender or design presentation is not necessarily a right to republish it indefinitely on a public commercial portfolio. Record credits and permissions for each image and each intended use.

Where subcontractors, previous architects, engineers or interior designers contributed to the outcome, describe roles accurately. Do not imply the studio designed parts of the finished building outside its scope. The project attribution and approval workflow provides a structured way to record authorship and permissions. For confidential or embargoed projects, follow the confidential project publication checklist.

This is editorial and operational guidance, not a legal opinion about a particular country's privacy law. For potentially sensitive work, obtain the relevant permissions and project-specific advice before making the material public.

Give the before-and-after page a clear subject for search.

The SEO opportunity is not to create a generic “before and after architecture” page and hope the photographs explain themselves. It is to publish a genuine project page that states the building type, architectural challenge, work undertaken and location if the project owner has approved that detail. A heading such as “Conversion of a former workshop into a family home” is more useful than “A beautiful transformation” when it is an accurate description of a real project.

Use unique photographs and place them beside text that describes the actual intervention. Google explains that it uses alt text, image context and other signals to understand images; its guidance does not promise that adding a comparison slider or repeating a city name will improve rankings. File names such as workshop-existing-east-wall.webp and workshop-completed-east-wall.webp can be descriptive when those names are verified. Avoid blanket renaming of already-public assets without managing references and redirects.

Write useful, distinct alternative text for each informative image. An existing-condition photograph may be described as “Original enclosed corridor with a narrow north-facing window”; the completed view could be “Renovated shared room with the former corridor opened to the courtyard.” Both are fictional examples: never copy them to actual assets without verifying the content. If a slider gives the two images the same generic alt text, a reader who cannot see the comparison may lose the change entirely.

Keep image captions and project facts visible as readable HTML. Add canonical metadata to the project page and link to it from the studio's main work collection. There is no special “before-and-after” rich-result guarantee, so avoid adding invented schema properties or claiming that a comparison automatically earns a visual Google feature. For the broader page structure, use the architecture project structured data checklist.

Store pairs as project content, not a fragile graphic trick.

A CMS record for each pair should identify the project, sequence, image IDs, state labels, dates where known, camera position notes, captions, alt texts, credits, source files, approval status and the currently published crop. This allows an editor to correct a credit or replace a low-resolution before image without accidentally changing its paired after image.

Example content model · entirely fictional
pair_id: REN-02 project: [approved renovation name] purpose: Show change to entrance and daylight before_asset: [licensed image ID] after_asset: [licensed image ID] before_state: existing condition / photo after_state: completed / photo camera_match: approximate; compare retained door frame caption: [specific visible change] alt_before: [describe actual existing view] alt_after: [describe actual completed view] photographer_credit: [verify per image] publication_approval: [named responsible reviewer] language: en / translated variants reviewed last_review: [date]

This is only a sample data structure, not a real project record, rights approval or completed commission.

Make the pair usable even if JavaScript fails. Render both image references in crawlable HTML or provide a straightforward static fallback. Use responsive image sizes and preserve aspect ratios to avoid layout shifts. The portfolio image optimisation guide

Before-and-after architecture portfolio publishing checklist.

  • The comparison has one clear architectural purpose beyond creating drama.
  • The project is correctly labelled as built, proposed or in progress.
  • Before and after images use comparable camera positions or the mismatch is explained.
  • Major retained and altered elements are visible in the relevant crop.
  • No image edit removes or invents critical architectural facts.
  • Material, daylight and weather differences are not presented as proof of unrelated claims.
  • The client and relevant image-rights holders have approved publication.
  • Credits, collaborators and the studio's own role are accurately stated.
  • Private belongings, people, exact addresses and security details have been reviewed.
  • The static image pair is usable without dragging or JavaScript.
  • Any slider is operable by keyboard and touch with meaningful labels.
  • Captions and alt text describe the distinct condition in each image.
  • SEO metadata reflects the real project rather than a manufactured keyword target.
  • Original files, web crops, language versions and approval records stay linked in the CMS.

The best comparison makes an architect's judgment easier to understand. It should be as careful with project truth, attribution and accessibility as the rest of the portfolio, while giving a potential client a clearer reason to explore the practice's work.
