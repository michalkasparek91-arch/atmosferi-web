How to review balcony privacy screens in architectural renderings

Review balcony privacy screen height, opacity, fixing, sightlines and neighbour views in architectural renderings without implying planning or safety approval.

Privacy is a viewpoint question, not a material swatch

A privacy screen can look convincing from a courtyard hero camera and fail its intended purpose when viewed from an adjacent balcony. This guide is about representing the approved design accurately in images, not proving legal privacy, planning compliance, wind resistance or fall protection. Identify the communication stage and keep unapproved details visibly provisional.

Collect the drawings that describe the actual screen

Request balcony plans, elevations, sections, screen schedules, adjacent-window layouts, terrace build-ups and guardrail details. Record drawing revisions and distinguish a permanent privacy panel from a movable shutter, planting or decorative trellis. If only a concept diagram exists, do not invent a fully engineered fixing system to make the CGI look complete.

Separate screen height from guardrail height

A screen may sit beside, above or behind a balustrade. Confirm the finished walking surface, screen top, lower clearance, return length and junction to the building. Do not extend a privacy panel to the slab edge simply because the camera hides the gap. A screen is not automatically a guardrail; refer separately to the balcony guardrail review.

Represent opacity and gaps without artistic shortcuts

Fritted glass, translucent laminated glass, perforated metal, vertical timber battens and opaque boards block views differently. Match panel spacing, transparency, colour and surface texture to the design schedule. Avoid using a generic frosted-glass shader as proof of real privacy performance; apparent transparency varies with light, angle and distance.

Test the view from both sides

Check the balcony looking outward, the neighbouring balcony looking across and a relevant public viewpoint. Keep the camera height and position documented. A telephoto street elevation may compress distance and suggest more screening than a person would experience. Use neutral crops to reveal gaps that landscaping or reflections might otherwise conceal.

Include the neighbouring openings that drive the decision

A privacy strategy is often responding to a particular adjacent window, terrace or shared circulation route. Confirm which neighbouring elements are factual and which are schematic. Do not relocate surrounding windows or increase foliage density to make a proposed screen seem more effective. If the adjacent building is uncertain, label the context as illustrative.

Keep structural and maintenance details honest

Posts, brackets, edge trims and returns have visual consequences. Show them where they are documented, but do not fabricate a concealed bracket or glass connection. Flag unresolved wind loads, drainage, cleaning access and replacement strategy for the architect or specialist; an attractive rendering is not a structural check.

Do not make seasonal planting carry a permanent promise

Climbers and tall planters can soften a screen, but their density varies with growth and season. Show planting as a separate landscape assumption and preserve the underlying screen geometry in at least one review view. If the project relies on vegetation alone, avoid describing the resulting privacy as guaranteed.

Review daytime and evening transparency separately

Backlighting can make a slatted or translucent screen appear more open after dark. If evening views are part of marketing, check them against the same geometry and document any illustrative interior lighting. Keep the night-time light-spill review separate from privacy claims.

Protect the design through image retouching

Image cleanup may erase slim posts, extend foliage, smooth panel joints or change frosted-glass opacity. Compare the final export with an unretouched geometry pass. Keep the camera ID, material revision and screen detail version in the review log. The post-publication change-control guide helps track affected images.

Fictional example: two neighbouring apartment balconies

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. Two apartment balconies share a dividing line. The concept CGI shows a translucent panel but hides its bottom gap behind a planter. A neutral eye-level view from the adjacent balcony reveals that the gap and a short return are not coordinated. The architect requests a revised screen elevation, confirms the return and records the panel finish as provisional. The updated image communicates the design decision without claiming certified privacy or planning approval.

A review package that leads to decisions

Export one elevation-like image, two reciprocal eye-level views and the intended marketing camera. For each, record source drawing revision, camera position, panel material status, visible gaps, adjacent context assumptions and unresolved fixings. Mark corrections separately from questions requiring design approval. Keep the final issue list with the image set; do not treat the CGI as a substitute for specialist assessment.
