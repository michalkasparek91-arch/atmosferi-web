Outdoor heat pump units in architectural renderings: a coordination checklist

A practical review of outdoor air-source heat pump placement, screens and landscape interfaces before residential CGI is approved.

Why outdoor heat pump units need a visual review

A residential exterior can look complete while omitting the outdoor unit that makes the heating strategy possible. A ground-mounted air-source heat pump can affect the garden layout, facade, routes, planting and the view from neighbouring windows. This guide helps a visualisation team communicate the current design faithfully. It does not specify a heat pump, establish noise compliance, or replace mechanical and planning review.

Request equipment and landscape information together

Ask for the latest site plan, external works layout, mechanical equipment schedule, manufacturer installation envelope if a model has been selected, elevations and any acoustic or planning information. Record drawing revisions and distinguish an approved equipment location from a placeholder. If the model is not selected, use a dimensioned indicative envelope rather than borrowing an attractive unit from an unrelated product photograph.

Locate the unit in three dimensions

Check the equipment footprint and height against finished ground, wall openings, terraces and adjacent paths. A low camera can conceal the equipment behind planting even when it is visible from the neighbour's first-floor window. Inspect a plan-derived view, an eye-level approach view and a raised view where neighbouring sightlines matter. Do not move the unit behind a hedge solely to improve the hero image.

Keep intake and discharge zones legible

Air-source heat pumps exchange air with their surroundings. Their required free spaces depend on the actual equipment and installation design. Do not place a decorative timber screen flush against the fan or fill the discharge zone with shrubs merely because the final image looks tidier. Represent any approved screen with its actual depth, openings and location; refer unresolved clearances back to the mechanical designer rather than declaring them adequate.

Model plinths, brackets and ground junctions

A unit can sit on a base, supports or a manufacturer-approved mounting arrangement. Its visual height changes when the base and finished ground level are shown correctly. Check any visible mounting feet, anti-vibration supports, paving edges and wall penetrations against the issued design. Avoid inventing structural or vibration-control details. A believable render is not evidence that the mounting arrangement has been engineered.

Show the condensate and defrost-water question

Outdoor equipment may produce condensate or defrost water depending on operation and conditions. If the design includes a drain, gravel bed or other water-management detail, coordinate its visible geometry with the landscape and drainage drawings. Do not imply that a perfectly clean paved surface resolves the issue. If the water route is undecided, log it as a design dependency. The rainwater downpipe review addresses a different, building-envelope drainage route.

Avoid implying acoustic performance through visuals

A dense hedge, slatted enclosure or garden wall can look acoustically protective without providing a verified noise reduction. Do not use a rendering to claim that a neighbour-facing installation is quiet or compliant. If an acoustic enclosure is designed, show its real size and apertures, and keep any performance statement outside the visual approval. The acoustic screen rendering guide explains the separate problem of representing documented screening.

Preserve service access and replacement routes

Ask the mechanical team which faces need inspection, removal or replacement access. In the rendering, check that proposed fences, planters, steps and bicycle racks do not occupy those zones. A unit tucked into a narrow side passage may disappear from the marketing image while still creating a significant access question. Use a simple top-down clearance overlay during review; do not present it as a manufacturer-approved installation diagram.

Distinguish a visual screen from an equipment enclosure

If a screen is part of the architecture, match its slat spacing, height, material and opening direction to the actual proposal. A closed box with no visible intake or exhaust is not an acceptable artistic simplification of an open-air unit. Where screening remains conceptual, say so in the image review record. For the broader issue of hiding roof-mounted mechanical plant, see the rooftop plant screening guide; this article instead focuses on ground-level residential coordination.

Check planting and seasonal visibility

A summer garden with mature grasses can hide a unit that will be exposed in winter. Confirm which vegetation is existing, newly proposed or shown at illustrative maturity. Do not use seasonal growth to imply permanent screening. In winter scenes, avoid decorative snow buildup that conceals the intake, discharge or maintenance face. If a scene includes snow or frost, its technical consequences should remain a question for the designer.

Separate the sales view from the coordination view

Keep the hero view that explains the architecture, but add one neutral equipment-focused camera that shows its distance from doors, windows, boundaries and planting. Use consistent geometry and drawing revisions across both. Record whether the unit is visible, obscured or outside the frame. A camera that excludes an object is not proof that the object is absent from the proposal.

Fictional example: a courtyard apartment block

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi project or a verified installation. A courtyard render shows an uninterrupted planted edge. A mechanical plan later identifies two outdoor units beside a side path. The visualisation team adds dimensioned placeholders and discovers that the proposed planting obscures a service face. The architect asks the mechanical and landscape designers to resolve the location before commissioning the final marketing image. The revised CGI reflects the agreed position, while acoustic suitability and drainage remain explicitly outside the image sign-off.

Use a short assumption register

For each unit record an ID, equipment model or indicative envelope, plan location, finished ground reference, source drawing and revision, visible screen, airflow and maintenance questions, affected camera IDs and responsible designer. Mark each item confirmed, provisional or unresolved. A missing model is a rendering discrepancy; an unapproved location is a design decision. Do not close the latter by retouching the unit out of the frame.

Pre-publication checklist

Confirm the number and location of outdoor units; current equipment envelopes; finished ground and mounting height; the proposed intake and discharge arrangement; approved screen geometry; water-management assumptions; maintenance routes; boundary and window relationships; planting maturity; and consistency across marketing and coordination cameras. Record unresolved mechanical, acoustic and planning questions separately from visual approval. The finished CGI should describe the design, not certify its performance.
