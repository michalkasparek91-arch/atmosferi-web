How to review trench convectors beside full-height glazing in interior renderings

A practical visualisation checklist for trench-convector grilles, floor setting-out, glazing interfaces, furniture conflicts and drawing-led approval.

Why a floor grille is a coordination issue

Full-height glazing often leaves little room for visible heating equipment. A trench convector can sit directly inside the floor zone beside the glass, so its grille becomes part of the architectural finish. A render that removes it for a cleaner composition can conceal a design decision; a render that adds a generic slot can invent one. This guide addresses visual fidelity and communication, not heat-output calculations or equipment selection.

Ask for the drawings that actually locate the unit

Collect the latest mechanical services layout, architectural floor plan, floor build-up, glazing and mullion setting-out, floor-finish schedule and the specified convector and grille details. Note revision dates and distinguish confirmed products from placeholders. A sketch indicating “perimeter heating” is not sufficient evidence for a particular grille width, length or profile. Escalate conflicting drawings to the architect and services engineer.

Set out the trench against the glazing, not the camera

Check the specified offset from the facade, start and end points, corners, column lines and mullions in plan. Perspective foreshortening can make a grille appear to touch the glass or float in the room. Verify with an orthographic crop before evaluating the hero image. Do not extend a trench around a corner or beneath a fixed partition unless the coordinated drawings support that geometry.

Model the grille and floor interface as a real assembly

A slatted roll-up grille, linear bar grille and solid decorative cover produce different visible rhythms and edge shadows. The selected assembly may have a perimeter frame and a distinct floor-finish termination. Match the approved visible dimensions, direction of bars and finish. Avoid applying a repeating texture to a flat plane when close-up views require actual openings and edge geometry. Do not infer structural depth from the appearance of the grille.

Review the facade-to-floor junction separately

The window frame, sill condition, facade base and floor edge can each occupy the same narrow strip in a camera view. Check that the convector does not visually cut through the glazing frame, waterproofing upstand or column enclosure. Render the documented interface, not an improvised gap. A convincing reflection in the glass cannot compensate for a misplaced unit. The glazing reflection and transparency review covers the separate optical questions.

Test furniture and curtain conflicts without inventing clearance rules

Place sofas, meeting tables, planters and loose furniture according to the current interior layout. Compare their footprints with the trench and note where a leg or plinth would obscure the grille. Recessed curtain tracks and full-height drapery can also overlap its apparent location; coordinate with the curtain-pocket review. Do not assert a safe minimum distance or airflow performance from a rendering. Ask the responsible designer to resolve any conflict.

Represent access and maintenance honestly

A beautifully continuous floor can hide whether a grille is removable or obstructed by fixed joinery. Where the selected system includes visible removable segments or access elements, depict them as specified. Do not add hinges, screws or access panels based on a generic asset. A visualisation can flag an inaccessible-looking location, but maintenance feasibility, loading and installation instructions belong to the product supplier and engineering team.

Keep slat spacing and materials consistent across views

A bronze-toned grille should not become chrome in a close-up or dissolve into black floor grout in a wide shot. Use the approved colour and surface treatment, plausible edge highlights and a consistent model across stills and animations. If the selected finish is unknown, mark the image as provisional rather than presenting a photorealistic material as a signed-off selection. Check reflections in adjacent glazing for continuity.

Do not confuse a trench convector with a ventilation slot

A floor-level ventilation diffuser, passive perimeter slot and heated trench convector can look similar from above, yet their coordination and specification differ. Identify the actual equipment in the mechanical schedule. The interior diffuser review addresses ventilation outlets, not perimeter heating. Never label a grille as a heating device merely because it sits next to a window.

Use three complementary approval views

Prepare a dimensioned plan crop showing the facade line and trench extents, an eye-level interior view showing its effect on the space, and a close detail crop showing the grille-to-floor edge. Keep camera IDs and model revisions in the review log. If a marketing crop hides the unit, retain a technical inspection view so its presence and position can still be approved. A single beauty image is insufficient evidence for a long perimeter installation.

Fictional example: a glazed co-working lounge

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. A fictional lounge has full-height glazing and timber flooring. An early rendering shows an uninterrupted timber border beside the facade, while a later mechanical drawing identifies a trench-convector run interrupted by a column. The visualiser adds the documented grille, checks its alignment against the mullion grid, and records an unresolved conflict with a fixed bench. The bench is not moved until the architect issues a coordinated decision.

Issue register and pre-publication approval boundary

For each unit, record its ID, governing drawing and revision, specified grille type, finish status, floor-edge detail, facade offset, affected furniture and camera IDs. Mark every question as visual correction, design decision or unresolved specification. Before release, compare the plan, hero and detail views; confirm consistency with the latest drawings; and regenerate all affected outputs after approval. Image sign-off does not certify thermal output, airflow, acoustic performance, structural capacity, accessibility or maintenance compliance.

Review the floor perimeter before approving the image

Share the current architectural, mechanical and finish drawings with the intended camera views for a source-led interior visualisation review.
