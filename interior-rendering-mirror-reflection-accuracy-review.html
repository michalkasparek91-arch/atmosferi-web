How to review mirror reflections in interior renderings

A mirror can make an interior image more compelling while quietly showing a room that does not exist. This review method separates optical plausibility, approved design and post-production.

Decide what the mirror needs to communicate

A mirror is not merely a shiny rectangle in an interior image. It can reveal the room behind the camera, a doorway outside the frame, the position of a luminaire or a person who is otherwise invisible. Decide whether the view is an atmospheric presentation or a geometry-sensitive design review. For a marketing image, the reflection should still agree with the approved room; for a coordination image, identify which architectural facts the reflection is intended to verify. A rendering is not a measured optical survey.

Check the reflected room against the current model

Use the approved room revision as the source of truth. Compare the reflection with the actual wall openings, ceiling edges, cabinetry, door swings and furniture positions. A common compositing shortcut is to paste a second attractive interior photograph into the mirror; this may introduce a window or fixture that does not exist. Ask for a diagnostic render with the mirror material enabled and the same camera, then compare it with the retouched final. Any change to the reflected architecture needs design approval, not just an aesthetic sign-off.

Record the camera and mirror plane

For a planar mirror, the reflection follows the angle of the mirror surface and the viewpoint. Moving the camera a small distance can change what appears in the glass dramatically. Record the camera ID, position, field of view and mirror orientation before approval. Do not solve an inconvenient reflection by moving the mirror plane without checking the drawing. A second camera at ordinary eye height can help reviewers understand what a visitor would actually see.

Trace unexpected sightlines and privacy

A bathroom mirror may reveal an adjacent room; a lobby mirror may expose a reception desk, service area or a neighbouring apartment. Check what the reflected view reveals about privacy, access and circulation. Where a reflection shows people, confirm that their presence is appropriate to the use of the image and that they do not conceal an important architectural element. Reflections can also expose safety or accessibility features hidden in the main camera, but should never be used as proof of regulatory compliance.

Inspect reflected scale and alignment

Mirror reflections can make spaces appear wider or deeper. Compare vertical lines, floor joints and reflected furniture proportions with the underlying model. Watch for stretched sofas, duplicated chairs, floating light fittings and reflections that do not meet the mirror frame correctly. Check that the mirror has the dimensions and installation height specified in the design. If the mirror is an illustrative furnishing rather than a specified product, state that distinction.

Separate light sources from artificial glow

A reflected pendant or window should agree with the light sources visible elsewhere in the scene. Look for missing reflected luminaires, duplicated highlights, inconsistent shadows and overly bright mirror surfaces. Bloom and exposure adjustments may be useful for atmosphere, but should not create a false lighting arrangement. For mixed daylight and electric lighting, use the interior lighting review to keep the time of day and switching scenario coherent.

Distinguish mirrors from glazing and polished finishes

A mirror, a tinted glass panel and a polished metal surface behave differently. Confirm the material specified in the schedule before treating every reflective surface as a perfect mirror. Review frame thickness, bevels, backing, tint and any edge detailing that affects the final appearance. If the specification is still open, label the finish provisional and avoid implying that the rendered surface establishes an approved product.

Audit retouching and AI-assisted reflection edits

Generative fill may produce a plausible-looking but impossible room inside a mirror. Retouchers may also remove a camera rig, a reflected person or a distracting door. Keep a before-and-after crop for substantive changes, identify the approved source model and record who accepted the edit. The image provenance guide explains how to separate presentation adjustments from changes that invent architectural facts. A polished reflection is not evidence that the geometry is correct.

Use a three-pass review

First inspect a neutral diagnostic render with the mirror surface, camera and room geometry locked. Second compare the final colour-graded image at the same crop and zoom, marking any difference in openings, objects and luminaires. Third have the architect or interior designer approve the architectural content while the publication owner checks captions, people and image rights. If the camera or room revision changes, repeat the affected passes. Keep the diagnostic image alongside the final export rather than relying on memory.

Fictional example: a compact apartment entrance

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A proposed entrance mirror is mounted beside a wardrobe. In the first beauty render it reflects a bright window, making the hallway appear open. The approved plan shows that the window is behind an intervening wall and cannot be visible from the chosen camera. The reviewer requests a diagnostic reflection from the current 3D model, checks the mirror angle and removes the invented window. The final caption describes the furniture as illustrative. The decision is logged against the plan revision and camera ID.

Keep a compact reflection decision log

A useful review record identifies the image, room and camera; the approved drawing or model revision; mirror dimensions and angle; the reflected elements checked; the mismatch; and the person approving the correction. For example, a fictional record might read: “Image INT-03, camera C2, mirror M1, model revision R4: reflected door omitted; restore from model; interior architect approved 5 November 2026.” This is a workflow illustration, not a claimed project result.

Final mirror-reflection checklist

  • Mirror position, dimensions and orientation match the approved design or are marked provisional.
  • Reflected doors, windows, furniture and ceiling details agree with the model revision.
  • Camera ID and field of view are recorded for the approved export.
  • Reflected people, private areas and circulation routes have been reviewed.
  • Lighting and material behaviour are consistent with the rest of the scene.
  • Retouching and AI-assisted changes are traceable and do not invent design facts.
  • Architectural sign-off and publication sign-off refer to the same final file.
