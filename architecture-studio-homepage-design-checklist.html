How to design an architecture studio homepage that wins the right enquiries.

A practical homepage framework for architecture practices: distinctive hero, selected projects, credible services and a clear first enquiry. Use it without turning the studio into a generic marketing template.

By Michal Kašpárek · Architect and visualisation specialist24 November 2026
In this guideWhat the homepage must do Opening screen Selected projects Service positioning Real evidence First enquiry Suggested section order SEO and accessibility Publication checklist

The homepage should help a potential client recognise the right studio.

An architecture practice homepage has a harder job than a portfolio grid. It must explain who the studio works for, what kind of work it undertakes and why the visible projects are relevant to the next visitor. A beautiful project image demonstrates taste, but it does not necessarily answer whether the practice handles renovations, public buildings, interior fit-outs or feasibility studies.

Start with the most important visitor question: “Is this an architecture studio that could help with my project?” The answer depends on audience. A private homeowner may want residential experience and a clear first-contact path. A developer may need evidence of scale, project delivery and coordination. An institutional client may prioritise relevant building types and procurement experience. Not every practice should lead with the same message.

The homepage is not obliged to tell the entire story. Think of it as a carefully edited introduction that points toward fuller service, project and studio pages. Try writing one sentence describing your actual practice before choosing the hero animation. If the sentence could equally describe fifty unrelated firms, it probably needs greater specificity.

Do not invent a niche, scale or award to appear more established. A small independent architect can present a compelling practice through a clear design philosophy, credible project evidence and an accessible first conversation. A credible limitation is better than a broad claim the portfolio cannot support.

Make the first screen visually distinctive and immediately understandable.

A full-bleed photograph, a carefully edited architecture film or a typography-led introduction can all work. The choice should follow what visitors need to understand, not a rule that every premium studio requires autoplay video. Strong hero composition and clear content do not conflict: the visual can communicate the work while a concise heading names the practice and its specialism.

Consider two layers: an expressive headline that captures the studio's design approach, followed by a plain sentence saying what it actually offers. For example, a fictional small practice could say “Spaces that make everyday life easier” and then “Residential architecture and sensitive renovations for private clients in [verified region].” The second line supplies context the poetic headline alone cannot.

Put the first useful action within reach: explore selected projects, see what the studio does or send an enquiry. Avoid five competing calls to action. If an introductory film hides the navigation, makes text illegible or stalls the page before a poster arrives, the storytelling device has become an obstacle. The Core Web Vitals guide covers the performance side; the film playback checklist covers controls and reduced motion.

Use real photographs, permission-cleared visualisations and project details. A concept image can be a good hero, but identify it as proposed where the viewer might otherwise mistake it for completed work. A dramatic crop should not change the facts of the project or imply a view that does not exist.

Curate projects around relevance, not just the newest upload.

The first portfolio row should help the right visitor select the next page. Choose a small set of projects that collectively explain the actual work you wish to do. That might mean a recently completed home, a complicated renovation and an early-stage public commission. Three substantially different examples can communicate more than twelve indistinguishable thumbnails.

Each card needs enough context to be useful: project name, type, location where publishable, and status when a visualisation depicts an unbuilt scheme. The project detail can then explain the problem, response and collaborators. If the homepage promises “adaptive reuse expertise,” link it to a real reuse project rather than a generic gallery. The architecture project case-study template provides a structured way to tell that story without exaggerating the practice's role.

Preserve a coherent art direction, but do not force every photograph into an identical crop if that hides the architecture. A compact gallery can use deliberate proportions and a predictable rhythm. Test project cards on mobile, including long titles and portrait images. Keep navigation obvious instead of relying solely on an animated hover state that never appears on touchscreens.

Explain services in the language of a commission, not an internal department.

“Architecture, planning and design” may be accurate but leaves the reader guessing which project stages the studio will handle. A better overview describes the kinds of briefs the practice genuinely accepts, and which decisions its work helps the client make. For example, residential refurbishment, new-build design, interior coordination and feasibility advice are recognisable project needs. Use only categories the studio can substantiate.

Keep the homepage's services introduction short, then link to a dedicated services page where scope, outputs, exclusions and the typical engagement process can be explained honestly. Do not present specialist assessments, sustainability certification or legal sign-off as automatically included unless the relevant professionals and arrangements really exist.

For firms with more than one audience, group services by the problem they solve. A project developer may need early feasibility and presentation work; a homeowner may need a first conversation about a renovation. Avoid turning the homepage into several unrelated microsites. If the practice also offers expert reports or separate professional services, provide an obvious path without crowding out its main identity.

Use evidence of real work, not invented trust signals.

A useful homepage shows why visitors can rely on the practice. Real photographs, credited collaborators, honest project roles and a short introduction to who actually leads the work carry more weight than anonymous praise. If you have testimonials and permission to quote them, provide an identifiable, accurate source. If not, omit that block rather than fabricating a five-star strip.

Present awards, years in practice, geographic coverage and sustainability claims only where they are accurate and relevant. “Ten years of experience” can be useful if true; a named project with clear scope is usually more instructive. For proposed and competition work, label the status so the homepage does not imply that the building has already been completed.

A short “How we work” introduction can make collaboration less intimidating: initial brief, scope discussion, proposal and agreed next steps. Do not promise fixed timeframes or limitless revisions unless that is genuinely how the firm operates. The aim is to help a visitor predict the first exchange, not turn the homepage into a contract.

Photographer credit and permission to publish should be handled before launch. The project attribution workflow describes how to keep roles and credits precise. If some work must remain confidential, the confidential project checklist helps decide what can be published without creating fictional case studies.

Make the first enquiry easy without turning the website into a sales funnel.

Architecture enquiries are rarely impulse purchases. The homepage should make the next action visible but allow visitors to explore before contacting you. A calm invitation such as “Tell us about your project” can lead to a concise form or a clear email address. Ask for details that help you respond: project type, location, approximate timing and a short description. Do not make a prospective client complete a lengthy procurement questionnaire merely to introduce themselves.

If the practice offers an introductory call, explain whether it is free, what it covers and whether booking creates a calendar invitation. Keep a written-contact alternative, especially for visitors with accessibility or scheduling needs. Calls should be an option, not the only route into the studio.

Show a human response expectation only if it is realistic and consistently met. Ensure that forms actually send, confirmation messages work, error states explain what to do next and notifications do not leak personally identifying details to analytics. The architecture contact-form QA guide covers these operational checks.

A useful homepage sequence for a small architecture practice.

There is no universally correct section order. Here is a practical starting structure for a studio with a small, credible portfolio and an identifiable audience. It can be adapted for a sole practitioner, multidisciplinary practice or larger commercial studio. The important point is that every section answers a visitor question rather than filling a design template.

SectionQuestion it answersEvidence or next step
HeroWho are you and what do you do?Honest positioning, primary project link
Selected workHave you handled work relevant to mine?Three or four verifiable projects
ServicesWhich types of commission can I discuss?Short service summaries and deeper page
ApproachHow will the collaboration begin?Concise, credible process description
StudioWho will I actually work with?People, roles and truthful experience
ContactWhat is the easiest next step?Working form, email or optional call

This is an illustrative outline, not a claim about a specific existing practice. For a commercial firm, work and project types may need to come first; for an individual architect, personality and approach may be part of the primary proposition. Use interviews with existing clients and the studio team to test which questions matter most.

Fictional homepage outline · do not present as client work
HERO: Architecture for thoughtful residential change SUBHEAD: Renovation and new-build design for private clients SELECTED WORK: Home conversion / Compact new house / Garden studio SERVICES: Early concept / Design development / Project coordination APPROACH: Brief conversation, agreed scope, clear next steps STUDIO: Named architect and real professional qualifications CONTACT: Share a short description of your proposed project

The examples above are invented. Replace them with a practice's real services, projects, credentials and contact policy before publishing.

Build useful homepage SEO into the information hierarchy.

The page title, main heading, short studio introduction and project links should describe the same genuine practice. Do not use a poetic name as the only heading while hiding the building types and locations the studio actually serves. If geography matters, use real service coverage rather than stuffing a list of unrelated cities into the footer.

Use descriptive, crawlable links to relevant project and service pages. Google Search Central's linking guidance explains why meaningful internal link text helps users and search engines navigate. Google's people-first content guidance favours genuinely useful information over content made only to manipulate ranking. Neither guide promises that copying a particular section pattern will increase traffic.

Use one clear main heading and logically ordered subheadings. Semantic sections, accessible navigation labels and reliable focus behaviour help visitors orient themselves; see the W3C page structure tutorial. Maintain good colour contrast and make text readable over photographs. Keep the hero visual fast and stable, and do not hide the essential introduction behind interaction or animation.

If the website is multilingual, localise the actual services, location references and linked project context. Avoid automatically producing language pages with untranslated content simply to reach more search terms. The multilingual architecture website checklist covers language navigation, canonical URLs and content quality.

Homepage checklist before an architecture website goes live.

  • The first screen identifies the practice and at least one genuine service or project focus.
  • The homepage speaks to a defined audience instead of claiming to do everything.
  • The most prominent imagery is approved, accurately described and correctly credited.
  • Selected projects link to pages with verified facts, status and the studio's role.
  • Service summaries describe actual work and point to fuller explanations.
  • Claims about experience, awards and sustainability can be substantiated.
  • Navigation is usable on phones, with a visible path to work and contact.
  • Hero images or films have sensible loading, accessible controls and readable text.
  • Contact routes work, are not unnecessarily long and set a realistic expectation.
  • The page has a descriptive title, one logical main heading and meaningful internal links.
  • Mobile crops, long headings and language variants have been checked.
  • The published page has been reviewed by the practice owner, not just the designer.

A homepage succeeds when a relevant visitor can recognise the practice, understand a few real projects and choose a next step without guessing. An architecture studio does not need to sound larger or more fashionable than it is. It needs a clear introduction to its actual work.
