Field notes · Facade visualisation

How to review glazing reflections and transparency in architectural renderings.

A glass facade can look persuasive while showing the wrong transparency, reflected surroundings or opaque zones. Use this source-led review before signing off the image.

Separate appearance from glass performance

A convincing glass facade can communicate the wrong design. Reflection, tint and apparent transparency are visual properties in an image, not proof of visible-light transmission, solar control, safety or acoustic performance. Define whether the review concerns a planning view, client design approval or marketing illustration. Ask the facade designer which properties are fixed and which remain provisional. Do not claim compliance from a rendering.

Start with the glazing schedule

Request the current elevation, glazing schedule, frame profiles, spandrel locations and any approved material sample or manufacturer reference. Record drawing revisions, orientation and the design team's decision owner. A vague instruction such as 'more transparent' can conceal a specification conflict. If the glass build-up is not selected, describe the visual treatment as illustrative rather than naming a product or performance value.

Understand why reflections change across viewpoints

Glass reflects a different portion of its surroundings depending on viewing angle, illumination and the environment being reflected. A front-on view may show an interior while an oblique view reads almost as a mirror. Review a street-level oblique camera and a more frontal camera before changing the material. Keep the modelled sky, nearby buildings and vegetation plausible: reflections of invented towers or trees can distort the architectural story.

Control interior visibility without inventing occupancy

Interior brightness, depth, blinds and background objects influence how transparent a facade appears. A luminous empty interior can look like a showroom even where the actual project has partitions and furniture. Agree which rooms are visible, which interiors are provisional and whether blinds or shading devices belong to the design. Do not add residents or identifiable activities merely to make the image feel lived in.

Do not turn opaque zones into clear windows

Spandrel glass, insulated backing panels, opaque partitions and service zones often sit behind visually continuous curtain walls. If a render shows uninterrupted transparent glazing across those zones, viewers may infer openings that do not exist. Compare the elevation and section with the material map. Mark the spandrel extent and distinguish it from vision glass, even if the intended facade reads as one continuous surface.

Opening directions need their own check

Frame and glass appearance alone cannot confirm how a sash operates. For casements, tilt-turn positions, mirrored handing and swing clearances, use the operable-window opening accuracy checklist.

Verify mullions, joints and glass scale

Reflections cannot rescue inaccurate facade geometry. Check mullion spacing, transoms, frame depth, operable vents, corner joints and panel dimensions against coordinated drawings. Oversized panes can quietly remove joints; very thin frames can suggest a construction system that was never designed. Keep the glazing material consistent across adjacent panels unless the schedule calls for a deliberate difference.

Compare daylight and dusk carefully

A facade that looks transparent at dusk may become reflective in daylight because the balance of interior and exterior illumination changes. When reviewing both conditions, keep geometry, glazing zones and camera positions traceable. Do not force a bright interior in a daylight image simply to reveal furniture. Night images also need an independent light-spill review; see the night-time lighting guide.

Test a realistic sky rather than a flattering trick

Cloud cover, low sun and wet surroundings change perceived colour and reflectivity. Select a representative lighting condition for the communication goal and disclose stylised weather where relevant. Do not present a dramatic sunset as evidence of the facade's typical appearance. For an option comparison, lock the sky, camera, exposure and context before adjusting only the proposed glazing treatment.

Fictional example: a mixed-use corner facade

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A concept mixed-use building has retail glazing below a residential curtain wall. An early image makes every panel equally transparent and shows offices through a zone intended as insulated spandrel. The architect requests a revised material map based on the elevation and a pair of fixed-camera reviews. No glazing performance or product identity is claimed.

Use a fixed comparison set for approvals

Deliver a neutral daylight elevation-like view, an oblique pedestrian view and, only if relevant, a dusk view. Record the camera ID, lens, sky, exposure, material version and source drawing. Show before-and-after images from identical cameras. Ask reviewers to comment separately on geometry, transparency, colour and reflected context. This prevents a colour correction from being mistaken for approval of the glazing specification.

Bird-safe glazing markings need a separate review

When collision-deterrent markings are part of the facade design, review their actual pattern scale and location separately from reflections. See the bird-safe glazing visual review checklist.

Keep uncertainty visible in final exports

Include a concise note listing unresolved glass selection, provisional shading, simplified interiors and unverified surrounding reflections. Store the approved material reference and export version alongside the image. If the facade design changes after publication, locate every affected image and update it using the change-control process. A render is a visual communication record, not a material test report.

Glazing visualisation sign-off checklist

  • Purpose and audience are defined.
  • Current facade drawings and glazing schedule are identified.
  • Vision glass and spandrel zones match the design.
  • Mullions, joints, opening panels and frames are coordinated.
  • Reflected context is credible and not invented to improve appearance.
  • Interior visibility does not imply unapproved layouts or occupancy.
  • At least two relevant viewing angles are reviewed.
  • Daylight and dusk use traceable lighting assumptions.
  • Material options are compared from fixed cameras.
  • Unselected glass products and performance are not presented as verified.
  • Approval owner, source revision and final export are recorded.

Use physical samples and specialist facade documentation to evaluate actual glazing performance. Rendering alone cannot establish it.
