How to review underfloor heating manifold access in interior renderings

Check manifold cabinet locations, access clearances, skirtings and floor-heating interfaces before approving interior architectural CGI.

Why a concealed manifold still matters

A carefully staged interior can hide an important practical question: where will the underfloor heating manifold be accessed? The cabinet may sit in a hallway, utility room or joinery wall, and its door, depth and surrounding finishes can alter the composition. This review is for accurately depicting a coordinated design, not for sizing pipework or certifying a heating installation.

Collect the right source documents

Ask for the current mechanical layout, manifold schedule, cabinet detail, room elevations, floor build-up, door and joinery schedules, and relevant sections. Check the issue date of each drawing. If the visualisation model shows a blank wall while the mechanical plan locates a cabinet there, raise the mismatch before finishing materials or lighting.

Verify the location against actual circulation

Mark the proposed cabinet in plan and elevation. Check whether it falls behind a door swing, a wardrobe, a built-in bench or loose furniture. Do not relocate it solely to improve the camera. The architect and building-services designer must agree any relocation and confirm the installation implications.

Model the enclosure, not an arbitrary rectangle

Use the specified cabinet opening, frame, depth, recess and mounting height. Distinguish a flush recessed enclosure from a surface-mounted one. A narrow decorative line may imply a fully concealed system even when the selected enclosure projects into the room. Where dimensions are provisional, identify the visual as provisional rather than silently choosing a neat size.

Keep maintenance access visible in the decision process

A manifold cabinet needs an opening strategy defined by the project team. Test the proposed door or removable cover against adjacent joinery, trim and furniture. A beautiful hallway render with a large console in front of the cabinet may misrepresent the intended use. The heating designer or installer, not the visualisation artist, establishes the necessary access.

Coordinate pipe routes without inventing services

Pipe circuits may be concealed within floor construction, but the point at which they reach the manifold can affect boxing, wall thickness and adjacent finishes. Ask for the coordinated section and floor build-up rather than drawing visible pipes for effect. The rendering should not suggest a feasible routing solution merely because the pipework is invisible.

Compare floor finishes and build-up levels

Review the floor-finish schedule and threshold sections alongside the heating layout. A change in build-up, transition strip or room level can become visible at the cabinet wall or adjacent doorway. Use the floor finish transitions review for the separate question of material boundaries and thresholds.

Resolve the interface with cabinetry and skirting

A manifold door may interrupt continuous skirting, timber panelling or a storage wall. Confirm whether the architect intends a proprietary access door, an integrated joinery panel or a clearly identified provisional treatment. Do not paint over a documented opening with uninterrupted veneer grain. Ask for hinge, handle and panel joint decisions where these are visible.

Use neutral lighting to expose geometry

Strong grazing light can reveal an access-panel edge, while soft ambient light may conceal it. Review at least one neutral technical crop that makes the enclosure boundary and adjacent finishes legible. Do not use dark shadows, furniture or a foreground person to hide a clash. Lighting should communicate the selected design, not compensate for missing coordination.

Pair a wide interior view with a technical crop

The wide view explains how the cabinet sits within the room. A straight-on elevation or close crop reveals its opening, frame, skirting termination and any obstruction. Keep the same model revision in both views. Where the cabinet is deliberately outside the hero camera, record its reviewed position separately so omission from one image is not mistaken for design resolution.

Separate image corrections from engineering decisions

The artist can correct a misplaced panel, wrong material or inconsistent cabinet outline. The design team must resolve system location, enclosure selection, access requirements and heating performance. Maintain an issue register with the drawing reference, owner, current decision and image affected. An approved image is not a mechanical sign-off.

Fictional example: a hallway storage wall

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or verified construction detail. A fictional apartment render shows a continuous oak-panelled hallway. A later services plan identifies a manifold cabinet behind the proposed storage bench. The architect flags the conflict, the project team revises the joinery layout and the artist updates a frontal crop and the wide view. The example makes no claim about heating output, compliance or installation approval.

Release checklist for the visualisation team

Before issuing final imagery, verify the latest cabinet position and type, opening geometry, wall and floor interfaces, skirting termination, nearby door swings, joinery conflicts and unresolved service queries. Compare the technical crop with the hero view. Record the drawing revision and decision owner. If any key element is unconfirmed, label the image as a design-stage illustration rather than an as-built or installation-ready representation.
