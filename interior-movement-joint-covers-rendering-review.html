How to review interior movement joint covers in architectural renderings

A practical interior CGI review of structural movement joint covers across floors, walls and ceilings: continuity, clearances, finishes and approval.

Why movement joints are not ordinary floor transitions

An interior movement joint accommodates building movement at a location defined by the structural and architectural design. Its cover can cross a corridor floor, continue up a wall and pass through a ceiling. A rendering that removes the cover to achieve a seamless surface can misrepresent the agreed construction. Unlike a change between timber and tile, the position is not a styling choice. This guide checks whether CGI reflects coordinated documents; it does not design or certify a movement joint.

Collect the joint layout and cover schedule

Request the structural movement-joint plan, current architectural plans and sections, finish schedules, reflected ceiling plans and the selected cover-system information. Record the drawing revisions and whether the cover is specified, proposed or unresolved. A generic metal strip from an asset library is not an adequate substitute for a scheduled assembly. Where drawings conflict, refer the decision to the design team rather than shifting the joint to a convenient grid line.

Separate structural joints from finish-control joints

A tile movement joint, a screed joint and a structural building movement joint can appear superficially similar in a photograph. They do not necessarily share width, movement capacity or cover requirements. Identify which condition the project documents actually describe. Avoid labelling a narrow grout line as a structural cover, or depicting a structural gap as a hairline texture seam. A visualiser should model the specified visible assembly without asserting its technical suitability.

Review the floor cover in plan and perspective

Locate the cover against structural grids, room boundaries, doors and circulation routes. Check its orientation, visible width, edge treatment and finish against the approved detail. In perspective, a long strip can look wider near the camera; use a plan crop to establish its actual geometry. Do not move the cover to align with a tile grid or carpet seam unless the responsible designer approves that change.

Follow the joint up the wall without losing alignment

Where the design continues the movement joint vertically, verify its position through plasterboard, masonry finishes, cladding and skirting. Wall covers may sit proud, recessed or flush depending on the approved system. Do not extend a floor cover into a wall with an invented profile simply to make the image continuous. At a glazed partition or fitted joinery, flag any unresolved termination for architectural coordination.

Coordinate ceiling covers and reflected plans

A ceiling joint can intersect light fittings, diffusers, acoustic panels and bulkheads. Compare the reflected ceiling plan with the structural joint line and any product detail. A visually uninterrupted ceiling may be inaccurate if the cover is specified to remain visible. Conversely, do not add a prominent metal band where the approved solution is concealed. Cross-check both a ceiling-oriented camera and a floor-oriented camera using the same model revision.

Check doors, partitions and fixed furniture at crossings

The joint may pass near a door frame, reception desk, built-in storage or movable partition. These intersections need source-led coordination, not an improvised gap in a rendering. Confirm which elements bridge the joint and which are separated in the design documents. Do not imply that fixed joinery can accommodate movement simply because the model meshes overlap cleanly. Raise conflicts for the architect and relevant specialist.

Do not infer accessibility from a smooth-looking cover

A floor cover may be described as flush in a visual brief, but the actual transition, surface characteristics and movement condition require technical assessment. Render the documented visible geometry and avoid depicting an unapproved raised edge or an implausibly invisible strip. The CGI cannot demonstrate wheelchair usability, slip resistance or compliance with local access requirements. For the wider route review, see the accessible-route guide.

Keep fire and acoustic performance outside visual approval

Some movement-joint assemblies involve separate fire, smoke or acoustic provisions. A surface cover alone does not prove any rating. Request the approved visible details where relevant, but never add a fire-rating badge, technical claim or hidden seal design based on appearance. If the specification remains open, label the representation provisional in the review register and refer performance questions to the qualified project team.

Render material finish and shadows consistently

A brushed metal cover should not become mirror-polished in a close-up or vanish into timber in a wider view. Use the agreed finish, realistic reflectance and plausible edge shadows. A recessed profile should read as recessed without exaggerated black cavities. Maintain the same material settings in stills, animation and interactive views; differences should come from lighting and camera angle rather than arbitrary retouching.

Fictional example: a co-working corridor

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. A fictional office refurbishment has a structural movement joint crossing a corridor between two floor-finish zones. The first CGI treats the joint as a narrow tile transition and stops it at the skirting. A later coordinated detail specifies a wider floor cover and a separate wall termination. The visualiser updates the floor and wall models, marks the ceiling interface as unresolved and requests architectural confirmation. No fire, acoustic or access performance is claimed.

Use a three-surface issue register

For each joint location, record a joint ID, source drawing and revision, floor-cover status, wall-cover status, ceiling-cover status, affected camera IDs and decision owner. Add cropped plan and perspective images to each issue. Distinguish a modelling omission from a design conflict: the first can be corrected against approved information; the second requires an instruction. Once a decision is approved, regenerate every affected output rather than patching only the hero still.

Pre-publication checklist and approval boundary

Before release, confirm the joint position and type; visible cover dimensions and finish; alignment across floor, wall and ceiling; intersections with doors, joinery, services and skirtings; consistency between views; and the status of unresolved details. The visual sign-off confirms correspondence with the supplied design information. It does not establish structural movement capacity, fire performance, acoustics, accessibility or installation suitability.
