How to review kitchen extractor ducts and ceiling bulkheads in interior renderings

Review kitchen extractor routes, ceiling bulkheads, maintenance access and facade outlets before approving interior architectural renderings.

Why extraction belongs in the rendering review

A kitchen CGI may look complete even when the extractor route is missing. This is particularly easy to overlook with an island hob and a shallow ceiling. The visible hood, ceiling bulkhead and discharge point all depend on decisions made outside the rendering. This guide helps architects and visualisation teams identify unresolved geometry without pretending to design or certify the ventilation system.

Gather drawings before modelling

Request the latest kitchen elevations, reflected ceiling plan, sections, mechanical drawings, appliance schedule and facade or roof outlet detail. Record their revision dates and who owns each decision. Ask whether the proposed extractor is ducted to outside or recirculating. A product photograph alone does not establish the installation arrangement for this project.

Distinguish ducted and recirculating options

A recirculating appliance may require filter access and a return-air arrangement; an externally ducted extractor needs a coordinated route and termination. They can look similar in a marketing image but are not interchangeable design decisions. When the concept is undecided, label the review image provisional instead of drawing a flawless ceiling and implying the services have been resolved.

Trace a continuous route in plan and section

Start at the extractor connection and follow the proposed route to its termination. Compare every bend and change of level against beams, structural zones, light fittings and other services. A route that looks clear on a plan may clash with the structure in section. Ask the responsible mechanical designer to confirm duct dimensions, offsets and technical requirements; the artist should not invent them.

Show the bulkhead at the correct finished level

A dropped ceiling or local soffit can be the most visible consequence of the extraction strategy. Model its extent and lower face from coordinated sections, not from an aesthetically convenient box. Compare it with window heads, doors, tall cabinets and the apparent room height. If the design has not resolved its dimensions, show the area as a design query in a technical crop.

Verify hood position and geometry

Ceiling-integrated extractors, wall-mounted hoods and suspended island canopies have different visual and spatial implications. Match the approved appliance reference, visible recess, suspension and mounting position. Do not assume a flush unit fits into an arbitrary ceiling depth. The manufacturer and project specialists must confirm installation suitability; the CGI reproduces the approved design only.

Inspect the cabinet and ceiling interface

Look closely where ducts or soffits meet tall cabinets, wall units and decorative panels. A small offset can affect door clearance or conceal a maintenance zone. Review the interface in a neutral-material crop so reflections and shadows do not hide an unresolved junction. When joinery and mechanical drawings disagree, record the discrepancy rather than choosing whichever version looks cleaner.

Do not erase required maintenance access

Filters, fans and other specified components may need accessible service points. The design team must identify their actual locations and opening requirements. Check whether an access panel is visible in the selected camera and whether cabinetry blocks it. An attractive ceiling is not a reason to remove a documented hatch. See the ceiling access panel review for a related workflow.

Coordinate the exterior discharge location

For a ducted system, confirm the approved termination on the facade or roof drawing. Its position may affect glazing, cladding, grilles and the external architectural image. Do not add a generic outlet where it will be least noticeable. Specialists must assess discharge, separation and regulatory constraints. The facade ventilation grille guide addresses the visible external interface.

Review lighting and other ceiling elements together

Compare the extractor and duct zone with pendants, downlights, sprinkler heads and ceiling joints. A CGI artist may be tempted to move a pendant to balance the composition, but that can conceal a coordination conflict. Export an uncluttered ceiling crop and mark the unresolved locations. The sprinkler coordination review provides a separate check where relevant.

Use paired views rather than one hero shot

A kitchen-wide perspective communicates the room-height impact of a soffit. An upward-looking crop or section shows the extractor recess, joinery junctions and nearby ceiling fixtures. Keep camera positions and drawing references stable between revisions. Remove people and decorative objects from the technical version if they obscure critical geometry. A plausible view is not evidence of mechanical approval.

Fictional example: an island hob in an apartment

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or approved construction detail. A fictional apartment image initially shows a perfectly flat ceiling above an island hob. A mechanical sketch indicates a route across the room, but its section is missing. The architect requests coordination; a specialist later identifies a revised route and a local soffit beside the storage wall. The artist updates both the wide image and a ceiling crop. No ventilation performance or code compliance is implied.

Keep a decision register before final approval

Record the extractor concept, appliance reference, route revision, bulkhead geometry, access provisions, external termination and outstanding questions. Distinguish artist-owned modelling corrections from architect- or engineer-owned design decisions. If a detail remains unresolved, keep it marked provisional in review material. Approval of the image confirms consistency with supplied information, not extraction performance, fire safety or maintainability.
