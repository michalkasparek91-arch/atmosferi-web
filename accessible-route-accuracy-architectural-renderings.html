Accessible routes in architectural renderings: what the image must not hide.

An entrance can look welcoming in a rendering and still hide a step, a steep ramp or a missing landing. Use this practical review method to separate accurate representation from unverified accessibility claims.

Separate visual communication from accessibility verification.

A rendering can communicate an inviting entrance while concealing a critical step, a steep approach or a door threshold. A beautiful image is not evidence that a building complies with accessibility requirements. This checklist is for architects and visualisation teams reviewing what the image shows, what the design documents establish and what still needs specialist verification. For a more specific check of step counts and landings in rendered stairs, use the staircase tread and riser geometry guide.

First identify whether the view is a marketing image, a design review image or a formally assessed document. For a marketing view, do not imply that the visible route has been verified merely because it looks smooth. For a design review, show the geometry needed to identify unresolved decisions. Applicable accessibility rules vary by jurisdiction and project type; the responsible design professional must check them against current local requirements.

Request the right geometry before placing people.

Ask for the issued site levels, finished floor levels, entrance details, landscape grading, door schedule and relevant accessible-route drawings. A general massing model may contain none of the details that determine whether the approach is usable.

Record the drawing revision and units. Mark missing information explicitly rather than inventing a convenient ramp or flattening the terrain in post-production. Where survey levels and architectural levels differ, reconcile the datum before modelling the entrance.

Check the whole approach, not just the doorway.

Trace the route from the relevant arrival point, such as a public footpath, drop-off or parking area, to the entrance. Review changes in level, transitions between paving types, kerbs, drainage channels and landing areas. An image cropped at the door can hide the steepest portion of the approach.

Check longitudinal and cross slopes from drawings or a measured model. Perspective alone cannot establish gradient. If the exact route is not designed, label the scene illustrative and avoid depicting a finished accessible approach as a settled fact.

Do not turn a ramp into a decorative plane.

A ramp needs more than a visually plausible incline. Its geometry, landings, edges, handrails and relationship to adjacent paths may matter to the design review. Model the actual issued arrangement and do not delete a handrail to improve the composition.

Never infer a compliant slope from the apparent angle in a perspective view. Confirm dimensions and details against the applicable design standard and drawings. If the ramp is conceptual, say so; do not attach a compliance claim to a concept render.

Inspect thresholds, doors and turning space.

Door leaf swing, clear opening width, approach space, thresholds and nearby furniture can affect circulation. A camera that looks directly into a doorway may conceal a raised sill or the space required to manoeuvre.

For review views, include a second camera or an annotated plan when the critical condition is outside the first frame. Keep door hardware, entrance mats and adjacent fixed objects consistent with the documented design. Use the interior furniture and clearance checklist. For placement and readability of directions at the entrance, see the wayfinding signage visualisation review for a separate review of movable furniture and circulation.

Keep site furniture and landscaping honest.

Planters, benches, bollards, cycle racks and seasonal planting can narrow an approach. A rendered route may appear generous only because these objects were omitted or pushed aside.

Cross-check the landscape and public-realm plan. Show relevant fixed obstructions at the intended positions, and distinguish existing elements from proposed ones. If planting maturity is illustrative, disclose the assumption rather than silently treating it as an as-built condition.

Use camera positions that reveal conflicts.

A low, wide-angle camera can make a shallow ramp seem almost flat or enlarge the apparent width of a path. A high camera can hide a threshold behind the door leaf. Neither camera position is inherently wrong, but a single flattering view should not become the only evidence.

Pair the hero image with a straightforward eye-level review angle where the route, entrance and level transitions remain visible. If needed, add a plan or section with actual dimensions. The review should be possible without trusting a perspective illusion.

Treat human figures as scale cues, not proof.

A wheelchair user or person with a pushchair in a rendering may communicate intended use, but a stock figure does not demonstrate turning space, clearances or safe movement. Do not place a person on a route whose geometry has not been checked.

Choose figures proportionate to the model and avoid using them to conceal a step or narrow passage. Do not imply a tested user experience from a staged composition. The image should represent design intent honestly, without turning disability into decorative storytelling.

Create a visual evidence register.

For each important entrance, record the source drawing, the condition visible in the render, the verification owner and the unresolved questions. This makes it harder for a presentation image to outlive the design version it depicts.

Approve the design facts before the atmosphere.

Use a two-stage review. First confirm model version, levels, slopes, obstructions, door positions and visibility of critical transitions. Then review lighting, materials, people and atmosphere. A polished render should never be used to close a known design discrepancy.

If a design revision changes the entrance level, ramp or approach, mark the old image superseded and update every affected camera. For systematic revision tracking, use the rendering feedback and revision checklist. Keep a clear owner for sign-off and a record of what the image was approved to communicate.

Publish with a proportionate disclosure.

Keep the drawing references, model revision, camera list and assumptions with the final image. Where accessibility features are still under design, say that the image is illustrative and subject to detailed design. Do not use a blanket disclaimer to excuse a known false depiction.

A final image may be suitable for public communication while still being unsuitable for technical compliance review. State that distinction clearly. Formal compliance requires the relevant drawings, calculations, applicable rules and professional review, not a photorealistic perspective.
