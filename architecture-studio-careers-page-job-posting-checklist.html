Architecture studio careers pages: make vacancies clear and applications safe

A recruitment page can waste an applicant's time even when it looks polished. Use this checklist to publish truthful vacancies, receive portfolios safely and close positions without leaving dead application routes.

Decide whether a careers page is needed

A studio should not publish a permanent “We are hiring” headline when it has no open roles. Applicants may spend hours preparing a portfolio for a position that does not exist. Separate three states: an active vacancy, an explicitly optional speculative enquiry, and no recruitment. Each state deserves different copy and a different action. A careers page is not a substitute for a clearly dated vacancy.

Write the role as a brief, not a slogan

Describe the actual work, level of responsibility, working arrangement, expected location or remote policy, employment or contract type, and the application deadline if there is one. Distinguish requirements from useful experience. “Architect” alone may refer to a licensed professional, a graduate, a designer or a visualisation specialist, depending on jurisdiction. State whether registration or language proficiency is genuinely necessary. Avoid unsupported claims about culture, salary or progression. Where pay disclosure is legally required, follow the applicable rule and verify the current range before publication.

Tell applicants exactly what to submit

Architecture applications often involve substantial files. Specify whether you want a PDF portfolio, a website link or both; a sensible maximum file size; accepted formats; and how to identify an applicant's own contribution on team projects. Do not demand confidential client drawings, unpublished competition entries or copyrighted photographs without permission. A lightweight link submission can reduce the need to upload sensitive work. Explain whether cover letters, references or academic transcripts are required and why.

Make personal-data handling legible

A CV may contain addresses, phone numbers, education and employment history. Tell applicants who receives their data, the purpose of processing, where the privacy notice lives, how long unsuccessful applications are retained and how to request deletion or correction where applicable. Do not publish a blanket promise of GDPR compliance without reviewing the actual workflow. Keep applications out of general analytics, public media folders and shared inboxes without access controls. Avoid optional talent-pool retention being bundled into the act of applying for a specific vacancy.

Test the application journey on a phone and with a keyboard

A careers page must be usable without relying on hover effects or a desktop-sized screen. Test labels, focus order, error messages, file-upload instructions, colour contrast and success confirmation. Do not rely solely on a visual CAPTCHA. A job description in an image or inaccessible PDF excludes people who cannot read that format. Provide the essential vacancy information as HTML, even if a PDF is also available. Confirm that a failed upload preserves the other fields or explains how to recover.

Keep the vacancy state and the website in sync

Assign an owner to every job posting. Record a publication date, review date, deadline and closure rule. When a role closes, remove application controls and show a truthful status rather than silently discarding submissions. If the URL has earned relevant links, retain a useful closed-role page or redirect it to the current careers overview; do not redirect every expired job to an unrelated homepage. If using JobPosting structured data, publish it only for a real, currently available role, ensure fields match visible content, and remove or update it promptly when the job closes. An evergreen editorial guide like this one is not a job advertisement and must not carry JobPosting schema.

Fictional example: a graduate role closes early

Illustrative fictional scenario, not an Atmosferi client or hiring claim: a small practice advertises a graduate architectural assistant role with a closing date of 15 December. The team fills the position on 5 December. The vacancy owner updates the visible status to “Applications closed”, disables uploads, removes active JobPosting markup and keeps a short note on the original URL. The studio also checks its social posts and any links from university job boards. A late applicant can now see that the vacancy is closed instead of sending a portfolio into a dead form.

Use a release acceptance matrix

The following checks should be carried out by the studio's recruitment owner and web editor, using test submissions rather than real applicant data.

CheckAcceptance criterion
Vacancy factsRole, location, contract, date and requirements match the approved brief
SubmissionA permitted PDF or link arrives in the intended restricted destination
FailureOversized file, invalid address and unavailable endpoint produce clear recovery guidance
PrivacyNotice, retention owner and access permissions match the actual process
AccessibilityKeyboard, screen reader and narrow-screen path remain usable
ClosureExpired role no longer accepts applications or advertises an active JobPosting

Review after every recruitment cycle

Test the entire application path before each new vacancy, not only when the website first launches. Confirm the role's current facts with the hiring manager, test a valid submission and a deliberately invalid one, check notification ownership, and confirm how a vacancy is retired. Review stored applications and access permissions after the process ends. If the studio rarely hires, a simple dated page with a monitored contact route may be safer than an elaborate recruitment platform that nobody maintains.
