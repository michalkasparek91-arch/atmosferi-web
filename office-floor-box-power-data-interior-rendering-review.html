How to review office floor boxes for power and data in interior renderings

A drawing-led checklist for floor-box positions, furniture conflicts, cable access and floor finishes in office architectural visualisations.

Why office floor boxes deserve their own review

An office interior can appear ready for occupation while its desk clusters have no credible power or data access. Floor boxes are easy to erase in a beauty render, yet their positions affect desk layouts, circulation, carpet patterns and maintenance access. This review concerns what an architectural image represents. It does not certify electrical design, network capacity, accessibility or installation safety.

Start with a coordinated drawing set

Collect the current furniture layout, raised-floor or slab plan, electrical and structured-cabling layouts, floor-finish schedule, box product details and any relevant workstation specification. Record drawing revisions and whether each box is confirmed, proposed or unresolved. A generic floor socket in a stock model is not evidence of a coordinated design. Where electrical and furniture drawings disagree, log a question rather than picking the visually cleaner position.

Match the floor grid, not just the desk centre

For the separate question of panel setting-out, perimeter cuts and thresholds, see the raised access floor panel visual QA guide.

In a raised-access floor, panel modules, pedestal locations and allowable cut-outs may constrain box placement. In a solid floor, recesses and service routes depend on another set of design decisions. The rendering team should not assume that a box can be moved freely to sit beneath a desk leg. Check the approved setting-out dimensions and show the visible cover in the intended location without inventing hidden floor construction.

Check usable access with furniture in place

A box may align perfectly with the drawing grid but become inaccessible beneath a fixed pedestal, filing cabinet or meeting table base. Review the lid opening direction, cable exit, expected chair movement and whether someone can reach the device without dismantling furniture. These questions belong with the architect and services designer. Do not make unsupported clearance claims from a perspective image.

Keep trailing leads out of the implied design

A render that shows laptops connected by long leads across a circulation aisle can imply an unsafe or uncoordinated layout. Conversely, hiding every cable can suggest infrastructure that has not been designed. Agree whether the image represents a tidy unoccupied workplace, an occupied scene with visible connections, or a coordination view. Keep cable routes plausible and flag unresolved desk connections instead of silently inventing underfloor outlets.

Respect carpet tile, timber and stone interfaces

The lid, frame and surrounding floor finish should reflect the selected system. A carpet-infill cover behaves visually differently from a metal flush lid; tile edges and timber plank joints create distinct setting-out issues. Check the specified orientation and visible perimeter, especially when the floor pattern is prominent in the camera. Do not use an invisible cover merely to preserve an uninterrupted texture.

Do not confuse floor boxes with furniture outlets

A floor box, a desk-mounted module and a wall outlet solve different coordination problems. One may feed another, but a desk power module does not establish where the floor connection occurs. Ask for the intended connection strategy and show only approved visible components. The separate kitchen island outlet review addresses joinery-mounted devices rather than office floor infrastructure.

Review the layout at three scales

Use a plan or near-orthographic view to verify box locations against the workstation grid, an eye-level hero view to judge visibility and atmosphere, and a low detail crop to inspect lid edges and finish alignment. Keep all views tied to the same revision. A dramatic wide-angle image can hide a box behind a chair or distort the apparent distance to a desk; it should not be the sole approval evidence.

Separate rendering corrections from design decisions

If the approved electrical drawing shows a box that the model lacks, that is a visualisation correction. If the latest furniture plan moves a desk over an existing box, the location requires a coordinated design decision. Record the issue ID, drawing references, affected cameras, decision owner and approval status. Update stills, animations and tours together after resolution; the rendering revision checklist explains how to track changes.

Fictional example: a flexible office fit-out

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. A proposed workplace render contains two four-person desk islands on a carpet-tile grid. The fictional electrical plan locates one floor box beside a table base, while a revised fictional furniture layout moves the base directly over its lid. The visualiser marks the collision on a plan crop and asks the architect to coordinate the solution. No new box location is invented for the marketing image.

Keep technical sign-off separate from image approval

An approved image can confirm that the visible cover, furniture and floor finish match a specified drawing revision. It cannot establish circuit protection, data performance, load rating, ingress protection, fire stopping or the feasibility of concealed routes. Those matters require the responsible designers and suppliers. Where product selection remains provisional, label the geometry accordingly in the review pack and avoid suggesting a verified specification.

Pre-publication review checklist

Confirm drawing dates; reconcile electrical and furniture layouts; check floor module or joint alignment; identify the selected box type and lid finish; test access around desk legs and chairs; review cable exits and circulation; compare plan, hero and detail views; document open queries; and verify every affected image after changes. For broader floor-material boundaries, consult the floor finish transition review.

Coordinate floor services before approving the image

Share the current workplace furniture and services drawings, the floor finish schedule and intended camera views to identify unresolved decisions before publication.
