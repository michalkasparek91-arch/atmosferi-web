Why movement joints deserve their own visual review

Movement joints are deliberately designed interruptions in the building envelope. In a close facade rendering, a missing vertical break can make two independently moving surfaces look like a single continuous plane. Conversely, an invented dark groove can imply a construction detail the architect never approved. This guide is for visual coordination and communication, not joint design or structural assessment. Record whether the scene represents concept, coordinated design or as-built condition before judging its apparent precision.

Distinguish movement joints from ordinary panel seams

A cladding module seam, mortar joint, control joint and building movement joint may look similar from one camera but serve different purposes. Request the elevation key, facade setting-out drawings and specialist detail references. Do not assign structural purpose based on appearance alone. In the image review, label each line by its documented role, or mark it as unconfirmed. A tidy grid of equally spaced lines is not evidence that the building movement strategy has been resolved.

Build a source-of-truth package

Collect the latest issued elevations, cladding setting-out plans, sections at slab edges, material schedules and facade consultant details where available. Note the revision and date for each source. Compare structural separation lines with architectural facade breaks without assuming they coincide. Ask who owns the decision at interfaces between concrete, masonry, glazing and rainscreen cladding. If the geometry is still under coordination, the render brief should state which lines are provisional.

Trace joints through changes in geometry

Inspect corners, returns, parapets, soffits, balcony edges and recesses. A joint shown on a flat elevation may change direction at a corner or terminate at an interface. Check the three-dimensional model against sections rather than projecting an uninterrupted line across every surface. Review apparent alignment with windows, door heads and mullions. The aim is not visual symmetry at any cost, but fidelity to the current documented design.

Preserve credible apparent width and depth

At a distance, a narrow sealant joint may be only a few pixels wide. That does not justify removing it from a detailed hero image if it changes the facade reading. Equally, exaggerated black lines can make a light cladding facade resemble a tiled grid. Where width and recess depth are specified, model them from the detail and verify at output resolution. Where not specified, record the placeholder rather than claiming an exact dimension.

Review sealant, shadow and material continuity

Darkness in a joint depends on recess, sealant finish, surrounding material, exposure and viewing angle. Avoid a universal black stripe painted over the image. Check that adjacent cladding finishes and textures terminate consistently and that weathering does not invent damage. A realistic shadow is not proof of a watertight or movement-capable detail. For broader surface approval, consult the material-finish guide, but keep joint location and continuity as a separate decision.

Check glazing and facade transitions

At curtain-wall interfaces, an apparent vertical line may belong to a mullion, a cover cap or a movement accommodation detail. Verify which element the drawing identifies before assigning thickness or reflectance. At masonry-to-glazing transitions, check whether the render has unintentionally bridged the separation with continuous trim. A photorealistic image can communicate the interface, but cannot certify tolerances, fire stopping, weatherproofing or structural movement capacity.

Use a two-scale camera review

Inspect a full-elevation view for continuity and a closer oblique view for recess depth, corner returns and material transitions. A frontal view may hide an incorrect return; a dramatic grazing-light view may overstate joint depth. Compare daylight and overcast exposures to identify whether the joint exists in geometry or only as post-production contrast. Save camera IDs so a later drawing revision can be checked in exactly the same views.

Keep a joint discrepancy register

A compact review record is more useful than vague comments such as ‘make facade cleaner’. Include joint ID, facade zone, drawing reference, model status, affected cameras, owner and decision deadline. Separate ‘missing in render’ from ‘not yet defined in design’. This prevents the visualisation artist from quietly solving a technical coordination problem with a Photoshop line.

Fictional example: a mixed-material housing block

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or an approved facade detail. A housing block has brickwork at ground level and rainscreen panels above. A marketing view shows a continuous brick corner, while a revised facade elevation indicates a vertical movement break near the return. The architect asks for the documented break to be represented in the close-up and flags the panel-to-brick transition for specialist coordination. The revised image shows the known location without inventing sealant performance or an approved construction solution.

Separate rendering sign-off from technical approval

The architect can approve whether the visible facade pattern matches the issued design. The relevant engineers and facade specialists remain responsible for joint movement, durability, fire performance and weathering requirements. A rendering should never be cited as verification of those matters. When the joint layout changes, update all dependent perspectives and publication files, not just the drawing that triggered the change.

For version control after publication, see the change-control guide.

Pre-publication checklist

Confirm that movement joints and panel seams are not conflated; all visible joint positions match the current source drawings; corners and returns are coherent; widths and depths are not exaggerated; glazing interfaces are coordinated; provisional decisions are disclosed; close and distant cameras agree; and the issue register identifies unresolved technical items. Archive the drawing revisions, model version and final approved image IDs together.

Review the facade before the final render

Share current elevations, sections and facade details with the intended camera views. We can help identify what should be shown and what must remain marked as unresolved.
