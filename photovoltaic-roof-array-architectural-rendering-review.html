How to review photovoltaic roof arrays in architectural renderings.

Solar panels are not a roof texture. Use this review to check module geometry, placement, reflections and visible design assumptions before an image is approved.

Begin with the electrical and architectural design status

A roof with a neat blue-black rectangle may be visually persuasive while contradicting the actual photovoltaic design. Obtain the latest roof plan, module layout, mounting-system concept, parapet sections and equipment coordination drawings before setting up the scene. Ask whether the array is a selected system, a preliminary capacity allowance or a purely illustrative option. These statuses should not share the same unqualified caption. The visualiser can communicate appearance; an electrical designer must determine electrical feasibility, generation estimates and system design.

Translate the array drawing into individual modules

Check module count, portrait or landscape orientation, nominal module dimensions, row spacing, access corridors and setbacks against the supplied layout. Avoid scaling one generic texture to fill the available roof area: its cell grid can become physically implausible and conceal missing modules. A module array should be modelled as discrete units where visible, with repeat spacing controlled in real dimensions. Record which layout revision each image depicts, especially when roof plans and marketing visuals are issued at different times.

Coordinate pitch, parapets and roof build-up

For pitched roofs, establish the actual roof plane, ridge, eaves and tile or metal-seam geometry. For flat roofs, verify finished roof level, ballast or support zone, parapet top and any known tilt angles. A few centimetres of vertical offset may change visibility from the street; a raised mounting frame can change the silhouette entirely. Do not flatten tilted modules to make the image cleaner or invent a mounting detail. Where support geometry is provisional, show a clearly labelled envelope rather than a supposedly final bracket.

Show the conflicts a roof photograph would reveal

Rooflights, smoke vents, skylights, drainage outlets, lift overruns, heat pumps, access hatches, maintenance walkways and safety equipment compete for space. Compare the PV layout with the latest roof-equipment inventory. Do not erase a roof vent because it interrupts a module row. Identify which discipline must resolve a clash and retain that issue in the review log. The separate rooftop plant screening review covers equipment visibility and screening rather than PV-specific geometry.

Do not infer orientation from the hero camera

A rendering camera can make a south-facing array appear aligned with an entirely different roof edge. Verify project north, roof orientation and module azimuth from plans rather than the direction of the sun in an attractive reference photograph. If the project includes east-west rows, preserve their opposing tilt and row rhythm. Use the same georeferenced orientation across all views when comparing options. The sun and shadow accuracy checklist explains why camera composition and solar position must be treated separately.

Make glass and frames believable, not decorative

Photovoltaic modules are layered manufactured objects, not mirror tiles. Their appearance depends on cell pattern, glass finish, frame colour, angle and surroundings. Request a manufacturer image or approved material reference when available, and use it only with appropriate permission. Avoid uniformly glowing blue panels, perfect black voids and exaggerated white cell lines. Reflections should change with view angle and sky, but the array should remain legible without adding fictitious highlights. Check whether the design calls for full-black modules, visible silver frames or another specified appearance.

Review at street, neighbour and roof-detail scales

A roof detail can make every clamp and cell visible, while a street-level image may show only a thin strip above the parapet. Test one near-field view to verify module geometry, one realistic pedestrian view and, where relevant, an elevated neighbouring viewpoint. Keep lens, crop and camera elevation documented. At reduced brochure size, repeated fine cells may create moiré; inspect exports at their actual presentation dimensions. Do not hide a visually prominent array by choosing only a low camera.

Keep trees and weather consistent between options

A leafy tree, deep cloud shadow or strategically bright sky can conceal the modules in one option and reveal them in another. If the purpose is design comparison, keep the camera, planting, season, exposure and weather consistent. For winter visibility questions, review a leaf-off condition when appropriate. Do not use a single attractive sunset as proof of reduced visibility or architectural compatibility. Record any contextual assumptions that materially affect the reading of the roof.

Separate the image from energy and planning claims

A convincing rendering does not demonstrate annual yield, payback, wind resistance, fire safety, planning compliance or structural capacity. Those require the relevant design team and evidence. If a client wants to communicate an estimated capacity or sustainability benefit, request an approved figure and its source rather than calculating it from the image. Similarly, never present a visual option as a permitted installation without confirmation. Captions should describe what the image actually shows: proposed module appearance and placement at a stated design stage.

Fictional example: a compact apartment roof

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A small apartment building has a flat roof with a proposed east-west PV array and two rooflights. The first concept image uses a continuous black plane, accidentally covering one rooflight and concealing the row tilt. The architect supplies a revised layout with separate modules, an access route and preliminary mounting envelopes. The visualiser updates a roof-level detail and a street viewpoint, marks the mounting arrangement as provisional and leaves the energy-output question to the electrical engineer. No installed project, measured yield or approval is claimed.

Create a review record that survives later changes

Deliver a compact register containing the roof-plan revision, module schedule reference, orientation, mounting-system status, camera IDs, material reference, export date and unresolved coordination items. If the module layout changes after marketing material is released, identify every affected image rather than silently replacing one hero shot. The image change-control guide provides a practical update process. Keep the review notes alongside the approved exports so the project team can distinguish confirmed design decisions from visual assumptions.

Photovoltaic array rendering sign-off checklist

  • Roof-plan and PV-layout revisions are recorded.
  • Module dimensions, count, orientation and row spacing match supplied drawings.
  • Flat-roof tilt, roof pitch, parapets and support envelopes are credible.
  • Rooflights, vents, equipment and access corridors are retained.
  • Project north and module azimuth are not guessed from the camera.
  • Module glass, frames and cell appearance follow approved references.
  • Street and elevated viewpoints are reviewed where relevant.
  • Output is checked at the intended web or print size.
  • Provisional mounting and unresolved conflicts are clearly labelled.
  • No energy-yield, compliance or planning claims are inferred from the rendering.

A visual sign-off confirms how the proposed array is represented, not whether the photovoltaic system is technically approved.
