How to review rooftop plant screening in architectural renderings.

A persuasive roofline can hide unresolved building-services equipment. Use this review method to keep plant enclosures, parapets and viewpoints honest.

Start with a roof-equipment scope, not a beauty shot

A clean roofline is an attractive marketing image, but a building's technical equipment may be visible from the street or nearby upper floors. Before commissioning a rendering, ask the architect and building-services designer for the latest roof plan, equipment schedule, parapet heights, screening concept and design status. Separate equipment already coordinated in the model from provisional allowances. A visualisation should not quietly delete a plant enclosure because it disrupts the composition.

Identify everything that can break the skyline

Check air-handling units, heat pumps, exhaust terminals, lift overruns, stair cores, photovoltaic arrays, maintenance gantries and safety rails. Their visual significance depends on height and position, not just footprint. The schedule should identify the responsible discipline, drawing revision, top elevation and confidence level for each element. If a unit's final dimensions are unknown, model a clearly identified envelope rather than a conveniently small box.

Use consistent height datums

Roof-level coordination fails when one model uses finished roof level and another uses structural slab level. Record the reference datum, parapet top, finished surface, support plinths, equipment top and screen top. Include any roof build-up or slope that affects the visible height. Do not infer these elevations from a perspective image. If dimensions conflict, ask for a design decision before producing final approval imagery.

Model the screen as a real object

A louvred enclosure is not an opaque magic wall. Its blade pitch, orientation, open area, support posts and colour can change what is visible through it. Solid screens may look cleaner but can raise ventilation, wind-load or maintenance questions that the visualiser cannot resolve. Obtain the intended screen system and indicative geometry from the design team. Render the screen with credible thickness and transparency, without asserting technical compliance.

Test views from streets and neighbours

A hero image from a low camera may conceal rooftop equipment while a neighbour's upper-floor window sees straight over the parapet. Review a street-level approach, a longer public viewpoint and at least one elevated receptor where relevant. Keep camera positions and lens settings documented. Where a verified photograph is available, a matched photomontage may be more useful than a free camera. The camera-matching checklist explains how to record those assumptions.

Do not let trees solve an unresolved screen

Seasonal planting can obscure a roofline in summer and expose it in winter. Do not rely on a conveniently full tree crown or invented neighbouring foliage to demonstrate screening. Keep the surrounding context proportionate to the evidence and, where it matters, review leaf-on and leaf-off conditions. A planting proposal is not a substitute for coordinated equipment and screen geometry.

Check night visibility without turning it into a lighting study

External roof equipment, maintenance lighting and internally lit louvres can become more visible after dark. If the project uses night-time marketing imagery, check whether the screen is shown with plausible illumination rather than a uniformly black silhouette. Avoid inventing operational lighting regimes. The night-time rendering review addresses visual assumptions about glare and spill; it does not replace a lighting calculation.

Separate planning, coordination and marketing images

A planning illustration may need to disclose maximum credible equipment envelopes, whereas a marketing view can communicate the intended completed appearance once the design is settled. Never label an unresolved placeholder as the final approved solution. Use a version register with the issue date, roof-plan revision, camera ID and approval owner. If the building-services layout changes after publication, revisit every affected view instead of updating only the most prominent image.

Fictional example: a residential roof terrace

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. Imagine a residential building with a roof terrace, a lift overrun and two heat-pump units. The initial render hides the units behind a parapet, but the coordinated roof plan places them on raised supports. A second view from an adjacent upper floor reveals the units above the parapet. The architect requests a screen study, while the engineer checks ventilation and access separately. The visualisation team shows the revised screen as proposed, not as technically approved.

Make uncertainty legible in the handover

Provide an annotated roof plan, a schedule of equipment assumptions, camera thumbnails, model revision and final export IDs. Note which screen dimensions are provisional and who must confirm them. If a viewer might mistake an illustrative screen for a permitted or engineered solution, add a short caption adjacent to the image. Architectural visualisation supports communication and review; it does not verify acoustic performance, ventilation, structural loads, planning permission or maintenance access.

Rooftop visibility sign-off checklist

Before release, confirm the roof equipment inventory; elevations and datums; plinth and parapet heights; lift and stair overruns; screen thickness and openness; street and elevated viewpoints; seasonal context; night assumptions where applicable; drawing revisions; and the decision owner. Record unresolved items plainly. A believable image is not evidence that the roof has been technically coordinated.
