How to review architectural rendering colours across screens

The same architectural image can look different in a browser, on a phone and on a review monitor. Use this evidence-based workflow to approve the digital deliverable without mistaking a display difference for a design change.

Define the decision before judging colour

Architects often approve a rendering on a calibrated desktop, only to see a warmer façade on a colleague's laptop or a darker lobby on a phone. The immediate temptation is to adjust the file until every device looks identical. That is not a realistic target: different displays have different brightness, gamut, viewing conditions and operating-system behaviour.

First distinguish three questions: Is the rendering communicating the intended design? Is its digital export technically consistent? And does a physical product match an approved sample? This workflow addresses the first two. A browser image cannot replace a material mock-up or contractual finish approval.

Choose one controlled review reference

Agree a primary reviewer, a reference image version and a controlled viewing setup. Use a reasonably characterised display, disable aggressive automatic colour or night-shift modes, and work in a stable lighting environment. Note the display model and whether calibration or profiling is known. Avoid claiming numerical colour accuracy when the equipment has not been measured.

Record the intended output: a web portfolio image, an online sales presentation, a PDF reviewed on screens or an in-person display. A separate print preflight process is needed for brochures and hoardings.

Establish an explicit digital export specification

For ordinary web delivery, agree a standard RGB export, usually sRGB, and embed the profile where the file format and workflow support it. Export a master and a separately optimised web derivative. Check that both use the same approved composition and that compression has not altered gradients, dark areas or fine façade patterns.

Do not treat a filename ending in “final” as evidence of the export settings. Store pixel dimensions, file format, colour profile, export timestamp and image revision in a small manifest. For wide-gamut workflows, test the actual delivery platform before relying on appearance outside sRGB.

Separate tone mapping from display brightness

Contrast and exposure are creative rendering decisions; monitor brightness is a viewing variable. If a façade looks washed out on one device, do not immediately darken the master. Compare a midtone, a shadow with meaningful detail and a bright highlight on the reference display first. Confirm whether the viewer is seeing the same file rather than a recompressed preview.

High dynamic range presentation introduces another variable: tone mapping may differ between applications. Unless HDR is a documented deliverable, review the standard dynamic range export as the contractual reference and label experimental HDR variants separately.

Audit white balance without inventing a material change

Warm interior lighting can shift perceived whites and greys. A client may report that a white wall is yellow when the actual issue is an intentional evening lighting scenario. Compare the rendering with its agreed lighting brief, then inspect a neutral-light reference if the finish needs approval.

Keep exposure, white balance, light-source temperature and colour grading as separate review comments. The mixed-lighting guide covers the scene itself; this article covers what happens after the image leaves the rendering workstation.

Test the real browser and content pipeline

Open the approved image in the target website, not only in an image editor. A content management system, image optimiser or social platform may resize, recompress, strip metadata or produce alternate formats. Compare the browser output against the approved exported file on the same display at the same approximate size.

Check at least one desktop browser and one phone browser. Look for posterisation in skies, blocked shadow detail, clipping in bright windows and unexpected saturation. Document the device and browser used, but do not infer universal compatibility from two screenshots.

Make comparison fair and reproducible

For two design options, lock camera, time of day, exposure, output profile and presentation size. Otherwise stakeholders may select the brighter image rather than the intended material or design. Display the options side by side within one controlled review session, with clear labels for version and provisional finishes.

For disputes, request the original exports and compare them locally under the same conditions. Messaging apps often generate previews; the preview is not necessarily the approved file.

Preserve legibility in dark and bright scenes

Architectural mood is valuable, but a sales website must remain usable. Do not place critical copy over low-contrast sky or interior shadows without an accessible text treatment. Check that overlaid labels, buttons and captions retain contrast independently of the image. Provide useful alternative text that describes the depicted project rather than repeating promotional claims.

Review on a small viewport where crops can remove important context. A mobile crop may make a façade finish appear more dominant or omit a caveat shown on desktop. Preserve disclosures about unconfirmed design elements near the image in both layouts.

Log a colour concern as evidence, not taste alone

A useful review comment identifies the image ID, export revision, display or browser, affected area, observed difference and requested decision. “Too orange” is hard to reproduce; “the specified neutral plaster appears noticeably warm in the approved midday view on the reference display” gives the team something testable.

Separate an approved artistic adjustment from a suspected pipeline defect. If the rendering artist changes the colour grade, record the revision and re-check the digital derivatives rather than silently replacing only one channel.

Fictional example: a housing façade on two phones

Fictional illustrative example, not a client project. An architect reviews a grey-rendered residential façade. The desktop image looks neutral; a phone screenshot looks warmer. The team retrieves the same original sRGB export, disables night-mode display settings for a controlled comparison and checks the actual website derivative. The web derivative matches the reference closely enough for the presentation purpose, while the phone screenshot remains visually different.

The team approves the version for digital communication and records that physical plaster selection must be checked against a sample. They do not retouch the entire project to match one phone. No colour tolerance or measured result is implied.

Keep an approval manifest with the image

For each released image, store its ID, source revision, export format, embedded profile status, intended channel, approval owner, approval date and any known limitations. Keep a copy of the exact approved file. If a marketing agency later applies a filter, changes the crop or creates a new colour conversion, treat that as a new derivative requiring review.

The deliverables handover guide explains how to keep source and output versions traceable, while the material approval guide separates visual communication from product specification.

Digital colour approval checklist

  • Identify the image version and the intended digital channel.
  • Agree a controlled primary display and review owner.
  • Export a documented sRGB web version unless another workflow is agreed.
  • Compare the approved master and published derivative on the same display.
  • Check shadows, highlights, gradients and material perception.
  • Test the real website on desktop and mobile without assuming all displays match.
  • Keep physical material approval separate from digital image approval.
  • Log the decision, limitations and exact released file.
