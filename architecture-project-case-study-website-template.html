How to structure an architecture project case study page

A project page should do more than display beautiful images. Use this practical structure to connect the design brief, architectural decisions and evidence without inventing results.

By Michal Kašpárek, architect and founder of Atmosferi22 November 2026

Start with the prospective client's question

To choose which projects deserve a prominent place on the studio's opening page, use the architecture studio homepage structure guide; this article goes deeper into each case study.

A portfolio image can demonstrate taste, but a prospective client also needs to understand the problem the architect solved. A private homeowner may look for evidence that the practice listens to a brief and handles a difficult site. A developer may look for clarity about programme, scale and team coordination. A public-sector client may need to identify the practice's actual role in a larger delivery team.

Choose the primary audience before writing. The case study should explain the project to someone who did not attend its design reviews. Avoid presenting every project with the same generic paragraph about light, materiality and context.

Open with verifiable project facts

Under the project title, offer a short, scannable fact block: project type, location at an appropriate level of detail, year or current phase, built versus unbuilt status, the studio's role and relevant collaborators. Include a meaningful size or programme only where the figure is verified and can be shared publicly.

Do not imply that a competition entry became a completed building. If a project is confidential, a broader location such as a region may be preferable to an exact address. The portfolio confidentiality checklist covers publication approvals in more detail.

Write the case around a real design decision

A useful case study has three connected parts: the constraint, the response and what the available evidence shows. The constraint might be a steep site, an existing structural grid, daylight restrictions or a need to adapt a building while occupied. The response should describe the actual design choice, not merely praise the final appearance.

For a completed commission, explain the delivered outcome without inventing performance figures or client testimonials. For a proposal, describe what the scheme aims to achieve. “The design proposes a courtyard to bring daylight into the centre” is different from claiming a measured daylight improvement.

Give each image a specific job

For renovations where an existing-condition photograph can explain the design change, use the before-and-after renovation photography guide to plan comparable views, approvals and captions.

Order images by the questions they answer, not by export date. An opening photograph or rendering establishes the whole project; a site or circulation view explains the setting; a plan or section reveals spatial decisions; close-up details show how material and construction choices support the idea. Avoid five near-identical hero angles.

For completed projects, distinguish photography from earlier visualisations. For unbuilt projects, label visualisations as proposed or illustrative. Keep important architecture in the crop at mobile widths and preserve access to the full composition when needed. The portfolio image optimisation guide covers delivery formats and responsive image performance.

Use drawings to explain, not decorate

A small plan can tell a clearer story than another atmospheric image, provided it can actually be read. Make a web-specific export with enough contrast, a simple legend and limited labels. If the plan depends on fine annotations, link to a larger accessible version rather than compressing an entire technical drawing into a narrow phone column.

Pair a drawing with the sentence it supports. For example, show the circulation diagram next to the explanation of how public and private uses are separated. Keep drawings consistent with the published design revision; a beautiful render and an obsolete plan should not sit beside one another as if they match.

Separate your role from the wider team's work

Clearly state whether the practice led the design, contributed a project phase, provided visualisations, collaborated on the interior or joined a competition team. Credit photographers, architects and specialist partners according to the permissions and agreements that apply. Readers should not have to infer authorship from a logo placed beneath somebody else's photographs.

If work is shared across practices, the contribution can be described precisely without weakening the project. Use the project attribution and approval guide to organise credits before publication.

Be precise about results and project status

A built project may have completion photography, awards, occupancy information or documented performance. Include only evidence you can support, with its source and timeframe where relevant. A planning consent, a shortlist and a construction completion are different milestones. Do not turn a proposal's ambition into a verified result.

For work in progress, use a clear status such as “concept design”, “planning submission”, “under construction” or “completed”, based on the actual project record. Update it when the status changes. If visualisations show a superseded design, either replace them or identify the earlier phase.

Offer the right next action without turning the page into an advert

A useful case study can end with one relevant invitation: discuss a similar project, view related work or contact the practice about a particular building type. Keep the contact path easy to find on mobile. A generic “learn more” link with no destination is not much help.

For a practice serving different audiences, the related-work links should follow the case's actual expertise. A residential refurbishment could point to another transformation project; a workplace case could lead to an office portfolio. Avoid placing several competing contact forms inside the same story. The contact-form QA guide covers what happens after an enquiry is submitted.

Make the project understandable to search engines and people

Use a descriptive page title and one visible heading naming the actual project and type. Write a unique summary, meaningful image alternative text where the image conveys information, and clear internal links from the portfolio index and relevant service pages. Do not stuff the same city and building-type phrase into every paragraph.

Keep one indexable canonical URL for each real project page. If a project moves during a website redesign, plan redirects rather than losing its established URL. Structured data should match the visible facts; it cannot substitute for accurate content. See the structured-data review and the URL migration checklist.

The following structure is a starting point, not a requirement to fill every slot. A compact house extension may need four sections; a complex adaptive reuse project may justify a longer narrative and several drawings.

Fictional example: a small library conversion

Fictional illustrative example only. This is not an Atmosferi commission, delivered building or client testimonial. Imagine a local practice preparing a case study about converting an unused ground-floor shop into a proposed neighbourhood library. Instead of saying “a timeless balance of old and new”, the page explains that the existing column grid constrained shelf layout and that the proposed central aisle keeps sightlines open between the entry and reading area.

The gallery uses one street-level proposed render, a simplified plan and a close-up of the retained structural columns. The fact block says “concept design” and credits the practice's actual hypothetical role as design architect. It does not claim opening-day attendance or a completed building. The final link invites an enquiry about adaptive reuse rather than promising outcomes.

Pre-publication review checklist

Before launch, have the project lead and the person responsible for publishing check the same final page. Confirm the project title and status, the contribution of each collaborator, photography and visualisation permissions, any sensitive address or occupant information, current drawing revisions and every factual claim. Inspect the story and images on a narrow phone screen as well as desktop.

Check that internal links reach existing pages, the project is accessible from the relevant portfolio section, and the contact path works. Revisit live projects after a documented milestone rather than leaving “under construction” visible indefinitely. For assembling original source files, start with the architecture website project preparation guide.
