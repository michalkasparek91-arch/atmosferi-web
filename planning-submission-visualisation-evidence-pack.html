How to prepare a traceable architectural visualisation pack for planning review

A view-by-view evidence register, honest image status and controlled approvals make a submission more useful than a folder of polished renders.

What makes a planning visualisation pack different

A planning submission image may be used to understand a proposed building's scale and its relationship to the existing surroundings. That is a different job from a marketing hero image. A persuasive image can be useful, but it is not automatically verifiable evidence. The planning authority, project type and jurisdiction determine which visual documents are required. This guide is a production and review method, not planning or legal advice.

Define the purpose of every view before production: orientation, verified photomontage, illustrative CGI, streetscape comparison or internal design discussion. Never describe an illustrative image as independently verified. The camera-matching guide explains the technical preparation of an individual composite; this article deals with the submission pack as a whole.

Write an evidence brief before choosing cameras

Agree who will read the pack, what questions the views must answer and which submission stage they represent. Record the proposal description, site boundary, relevant neighbouring buildings and any agreed viewpoints or assessment methodology. If a planning consultant or authority has specified a format, follow that instruction rather than an attractive default template.

A useful brief distinguishes required evidence from optional presentation images. It also names the person authorised to approve viewpoint selection, model geometry and the final issue. An image artist should not make a planning judgement by silently selecting the least revealing view.

Freeze a traceable source-information register

For each image, record the survey source, model version, drawing numbers and revisions, landscape information, existing-site photography and any assumptions. Include units and coordinate reference where relevant. Mark a missing datum as unresolved rather than approximating it without disclosure.

Use a small table with fields for image ID, source file, revision, received date, responsible discipline and unresolved issue. When a facade or roof changes, the register shows which views need to be regenerated. A rendered pixel is not a substitute for a controlled drawing.

Document how viewpoints were selected

A single camera can hide building height, roof equipment or a sensitive boundary. Explain why each viewpoint is relevant: public approach, adjacent street, neighbouring property, open space or a designated view, as applicable. Record rejected or supplementary viewpoints where their omission could mislead.

For photographs, keep original image files, location notes, camera position, lens information and date. Use the photomontage camera-matching checklist for survey alignment, lens correction and occlusion. A viewpoint plan with numbered markers lets reviewers connect each perspective to a real place.

Separate existing conditions from proposed changes

Existing buildings, mature trees, boundaries and levels must not be edited away for a cleaner image. Where site photography is out of date, identify what has changed and whether new photography is needed. Do not combine photographs from different dates without explaining the method.

Use consistent labels such as Existing, Proposed and Illustrative. If a before-and-after pair is presented, keep viewpoint and crop comparable. For retained versus altered building elements, consult the existing/proposed fabric review.

Test the model against controlled geometry

Before rendering, compare massing, roof heights, facade openings, setbacks, boundary relationships and ground levels against issued drawings. Record any departures introduced for visibility or composition. Avoid changing a parapet height or widening a courtyard merely to improve the frame.

A useful check pairs an orthographic model view with the relevant elevation or section and notes the revision. If an element remains conceptual, its representation and caption must say so. Planning reviewers should not have to infer whether a photogenic feature is part of the application.

Treat landscape and seasonal context as evidence questions

Tree canopies, leaf cover, proposed planting and weather can change the apparent visibility of a building. Distinguish existing vegetation from proposed planting and avoid depicting future planting as mature without a stated assumption. A summer-only view may not communicate winter visibility where that matters to the assessment.

Do not remove parked cars, street furniture or neighbouring structures merely to improve the composition unless the alteration is explicitly documented. The landscape accuracy guide covers species, growth stage and planting schedules in more detail.

Disclose lighting and atmospheric choices

Sun angle, sky condition, exposure and artificial lighting affect perceived bulk, material contrast and reflections. Record the chosen time and lighting scenario when it matters to the interpretation. A warm dusk view may be excellent for a brochure but should not replace a neutral view when the planning question concerns visibility or context.

Avoid using haze, selective contrast or extreme depth of field to suppress surrounding buildings. Where sunlight, glare or overshadowing is material, rely on the appropriate specialist analysis rather than an attractive rendering. The sun and shadow checklist addresses visual consistency, not statutory assessment.

Give each image an intelligible caption

A caption should identify the image ID, viewpoint, direction of view, proposal revision, date and status. State whether it is a verified photomontage, illustrative visualisation or diagram. Describe material assumptions only where they are not settled. Avoid absolute statements about visibility, daylight or impact that the image cannot establish.

If an image is shown outside the original report, keep its identifying caption attached. A standalone image shared by email should not lose the distinction between an illustrative concept and a submitted proposal.

Create a pack-level evidence matrix

Use one row per visual. Suggested columns are image ID, planning question, viewpoint reference, source drawing revision, existing-photo date, model revision, status, reviewer, approval date and outstanding qualification. This makes a submission auditable without burying every decision in a long email thread.

For a small scheme the matrix may be a single page. For a complex scheme it can be a spreadsheet linked to a controlled image register. The key is traceability: a reviewer should be able to identify which information produced a particular image.

Run separate technical and communication reviews

First ask the design team to check geometry, context, source revisions and visible omissions. Then review readability, labels, contrast and composition. Combining both into one subjective approval round can allow a technically incorrect image to pass because it looks convincing.

Record corrections by image ID and priority. When a design change arrives after visual approval, recheck all affected viewpoints. The revision checklist offers a way to keep visual comments separate from changes to the design.

Fictional illustrative example: a small infill housing proposal

Imagine a fictional proposal for six apartments between two existing buildings. The team prepares a street-level view, an oblique courtyard view and a location plan. The street image uses a dated site photograph; the courtyard view is CGI because access for photography was unavailable. These are hypothetical details, not an Atmosferi commission.

The evidence matrix marks the street image as a camera-matched composite pending survey confirmation, and the courtyard as illustrative only. A revised roof height triggers a re-render of both images. The final captions identify their different status instead of presenting them as equivalent verified evidence.

Package the final issue so nothing becomes detached

Issue a dated PDF or document set with a cover sheet, contents, numbered views, captions, viewpoint plan, assumptions and revision log. Keep master images and source records separately in a controlled archive. Do not remove qualifications when extracting images for slides or public websites.

Use a consistent naming convention, for example project-viewpoint-revision-date, and confirm the export resolution at the intended reading size. Check that legends and captions remain readable on a phone if the pack will be shared digitally; critical evidence should not rely on hover interactions.

Pre-submission checklist

Before issue, confirm that the purpose and status of every view are clear; source drawings and dates are recorded; camera locations are traceable; geometry agrees with the approved revision; existing context is not silently altered; planting and lighting assumptions are disclosed; captions stay with exported images; the evidence matrix is complete; and the responsible project reviewer has approved the final version.

A visualisation pack can help explain a proposal, but it does not replace the planning authority's required documents, specialist assessments or professional sign-off. Where the required evidential standard is unclear, obtain project-specific guidance before calling an image verified.
