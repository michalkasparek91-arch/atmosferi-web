How to prepare architectural renderings for brochures and hoardings.

A print brochure and a three-metre hoarding do not need the same export. This guide helps architects, developers and visualisation teams set image resolution, preserve composition, coordinate with printers and approve what actually goes to press.

Start with the physical object, not an export preset.

A rendering that looks excellent on a laptop may fail in a printed sales brochure. The same image might also be requested for a wall-sized site hoarding, a sales-suite panel and a presentation board. Those are different production problems. Before commissioning a final high-resolution render, ask where the image will appear, how large it will be printed, how close someone will stand and what must remain legible.

A brochure is held close. A hoarding is normally read from farther away, often while someone is moving. A backlit display behaves differently from a paper print. The image may be cropped by a designer, interrupted by a fold, covered by a headline or divided across panels. None of these decisions can be solved by adding pixels at the last minute.

Make one row per use in a deliverables schedule: finished dimensions, orientation, viewing distance, placement, printer or fabricator, deadline and approval owner. If a printer has not been selected, label the technical specification provisional and keep a flexible master. Do not promise a particular resolution or colour conversion before the output process is known.

Get the printer's requirements before choosing file settings.

Ask the printer or production designer for the actual finished size, document scale, required bleed, safe area, preferred colour space and ICC profile, acceptable PDF standard, image resolution at final size and whether marks or panel overlaps are needed. Some large-format suppliers work with artwork at a reduced scale. Others want a full-size file. The difference changes how you interpret a stated pixels-per-inch value.

Also ask about the substrate. Coated paper, uncoated stock, vinyl, mesh and backlit material can reproduce contrast and fine detail differently. A proof on one material does not automatically approve the same image on another. The print provider should define production tolerances, not the visualisation artist guessing from a generic internet checklist.

For a brochure, the layout designer may deliver a complete press-ready PDF while the visualisation studio supplies a colour-managed image. For a hoarding, the fabricator may request a flattened image or segmented artwork. Agree who owns the final print file so that the rendering artist is not accidentally held responsible for a layout or conversion they never saw.

Calculate image dimensions from final size and effective PPI.

Pixel dimensions and PPI describe different things. A file that is 6,000 pixels wide contains the same pixel data whether its metadata says 72 or 300 PPI. What matters for print is how many pixels are available across the physical width at which the image is placed. The basic calculation is required pixels = printed inches × target effective PPI, where printed inches = millimetres ÷ 25.4.

For a full-width A4 image at 210 mm and 300 effective PPI, the calculation is 210 ÷ 25.4 × 300 ≈ 2,480 pixels wide. At 297 mm high, it is about 3,508 pixels. If the image extends to a bleed edge, calculate for that larger image area, not just the trim size. If the designer places it across a spread, calculate the actual placement width rather than assuming it fits one page.

Render for the real crop, not only the hero image.

Architecture is especially vulnerable to careless cropping. A brochure designer may remove the entrance, a vertical social derivative may distort the relationship between buildings, or a hoarding seam may pass through the primary facade. Set a safe composition for each output before approving the camera. Show the actual page and hoarding proportions as overlays during preview, including headline zones and likely panel breaks.

When one image must serve several formats, render with enough breathing room around the building for planned crops. But do not widen the camera until room sizes or facade proportions look misleading. A slightly different camera or a second composition may be more honest and more useful than stretching one master to fit every shape.

Ask the layout team to mark non-negotiable architectural features: entrances, boundaries, street relationships, view corridors and anything essential to the design claim. A visual that loses those elements after cropping is not simply an aesthetic compromise. It can change what the audience understands about the project.

Keep trim, bleed, folds and panel seams out of the architectural story.

Bleed is extra artwork extending beyond a trim edge. It helps prevent a white sliver after cutting, but it does not make the important part of a building safe. Keep key details, labels and disclaimers inside the printer's designated safe area. For a folded brochure, review the fold and binding positions. For a large hoarding, mark the physical panel joins, overlaps, fixings and any cut-outs before approving the image placement.

Some print jobs use 3 mm bleed; others use different values or no trim bleed at all. Do not apply 3 mm mechanically to every job. The correct value comes from the print provider and depends on the production method. If the artwork is supplied at one-tenth scale, confirm how the specified bleed should be represented in that document.

For a clear distinction between document trim, bleed and output marks, see Adobe's PDF export options. The print designer should decide whether crop marks belong in the final PDF; a standalone rendering usually should not contain them.

Keep a dependable master before converting for a particular press.

A rendered RGB image is not automatically wrong for print. A printer or designer may prefer a tagged RGB image and handle conversion in a controlled layout workflow. Another supplier may require a particular CMYK profile. Converting early to an arbitrary CMYK preset can discard colour information and make later reuse harder. Preserve the approved master and produce destination-specific derivatives only when the output conditions are known.

Ask for the destination profile or output intent, then soft-proof the image under an appropriate viewing setup. Pay special attention to warm interior light, deep greens, saturated skies, dark glazing and near-black facade materials. These can change character when converted or printed. Adjust for the actual output, but do not disguise a design issue with grading.

When reviewing proofs, distinguish a colour-management problem from a rendering problem. A darker print might need an output adjustment, while a facade material that was never accurately specified needs architectural clarification. Record the approved colour version and the substrate used for the proof. A phone screenshot of the PDF is not a colour approval.

Check detail, sharpening and noise where the audience will see them.

Inspect the image at the intended physical size or a credible simulation of it. Fine facade lines, window mullions, balcony railings and planting textures can break down after scaling or compression. A render may look sharp at 25% zoom while revealing noisy glass, repeating textures or broken foliage when printed large. Conversely, excessive sharpening can create bright halos around roof edges and silhouettes.

Upscaling can help prepare a derivative, but it cannot verify missing architectural information. If an AI-assisted upscale invents railings, window divisions or facade joints, the result must be checked against the approved model. Never use a plausible-looking detail as evidence that the design contains it. If a critical feature is missing from the source, re-rendering from the model may be safer than inventing it in post-production.

Review in two passes: first at a realistic reading distance to check hierarchy and mood, then close enough to catch artefacts and incorrect geometry. The approval is not complete until the crop, sharpening and colour version have been checked together.

Make design status, image rights and captions part of the artwork brief.

Marketing visuals often depict buildings before construction is complete. State whether the rendering represents a proposed design, an approved scheme or a later revision. Identify elements that remain illustrative, such as furniture, planting maturity, neighbouring buildings or future amenities. Where qualifications are necessary, the layout must leave room for readable text rather than burying it in a tiny footer.

Check permission to use supplied photography, textures, logos, maps and any other third-party material in printed advertising. Permission for an internal design review or website does not necessarily cover an outdoor campaign. The project team should verify the actual licence terms, any required credits and the territories and duration of the intended use. Do not assume a file is cleared merely because it sits in a shared project folder.

Record the project name, design revision, image ID and approval date in the handover manifest. If a material or building form changes after sign-off, the team needs to know which brochure pages and physical placements depend on that image. The rendering change-control checklist covers that later replacement process.

A high-resolution render is not the same as a press-ready PDF.

A rendering studio may deliver a high-quality TIFF or other agreed image master, plus output-sized derivatives. The brochure designer then places those images with typography, diagrams and legal copy, and exports the press-ready PDF. Do not confuse the image file's resolution with the final PDF's correctness. A print PDF also needs suitable page boxes, fonts, colour output settings, image links and preflight checks.

PDF/X is a family of print-exchange standards, not a single magic button. Ask the printer which version and output intent they require. Adobe's print PDF export guide describes how PDF/X options are selected; the Ghent Workgroup specifications explain why print workflows use defined preflight rules. The final operator should check the exact exported file, not only the document preset.

For hoardings, the fabricator may need separate panels, cut lines or scale information rather than one monolithic PDF. Confirm whether they will split the file and where seams will fall. Provide the correct reference and version; avoid sending a folder of near-identical “final” files and hoping the right one gets printed.

The useful feature is not the invented project. It is the separation between the image master, each physical placement, technical assumptions and approval responsibilities. A team can replace every fictional value with a verified requirement before committing to final renders.

Keep the image ID stable across the rendering, layout and production folders. If a brochure is updated while a hoarding is already at the fabricator, the manifest makes that divergence visible before an outdated image is installed.

Approve a physical or contractually appropriate proof, not just a monitor preview.

Review the printer's proof or agreed test output under reasonable viewing conditions. Check the most vulnerable areas first: dark facade materials, shadow detail, skin tones if people appear, fine planting, sky gradients, labels and the relationship between the image and overlaid text. A proof may reveal colour or contrast issues that were invisible in the original RGB file.

For a large hoarding, a representative material sample at the intended scale can be more informative than shrinking the entire artwork to A4. For a brochure, check a spread with the real paper stock and finishing when feasible. Record what the proof represents and which properties it does not test. A soft proof is useful, but it does not guarantee the result on a specific press or substrate.

Require one named sign-off for architectural accuracy and another for production readiness. The person approving a facade should not silently become responsible for PDF fonts or bleed. Conversely, a printer's successful preflight does not confirm that the depicted architecture matches the current design.

Hand over an approved set that can be reproduced and revised.

Keep the editable source or layered master where the agreed scope permits, a flattened approved image, output-specific versions, a preview contact sheet and a short manifest. Use clear filenames with project, view ID, revision, crop and purpose. Avoid relying on colour labels or informal chat messages to indicate which file is approved.

For each file, record pixel dimensions, intended physical size, colour profile if relevant, creation or approval date, destination and any unresolved conditions. If the printer changes the size or substrate, reopen the relevant checks rather than simply renaming the old export. Where the image is also published online, prepare a separate lightweight web version; the architecture image optimisation guide explains that different delivery path.

A good handover does not promise that one image will work everywhere. It tells the next person exactly which file was approved for which use, and what must be rechecked when the use changes.

A final checklist before architectural images go to print.

  • The actual printed uses, dimensions, viewing distances and substrates are recorded.
  • The printer or fabricator has confirmed effective PPI, scale, bleed, colour and file requirements.
  • Required pixel dimensions were calculated from the image's final placement size.
  • The approved camera and crop preserve key architectural relationships.
  • Trim, folds, seams, headlines and safe areas do not obscure critical information.
  • The colour-managed master is retained separately from output-specific conversions.
  • Detail, noise, sharpening and geometry have been checked at an appropriate scale.
  • AI-assisted enlargement, if used, has not invented architectural details.
  • Design status, illustrative elements, captions and disclaimers are approved and legible.
  • Third-party asset licences cover the intended print and outdoor uses.
  • The correct owner has preflighted the actual final PDF or fabricator file.
  • A suitable proof or test print has been reviewed, with limitations recorded.
  • Each delivered asset has a stable ID, revision, intended destination and named approval owner.

Print preparation is not about demanding the largest possible render. It is about matching a truthful architectural image to a physical object and an accountable production workflow. Decide the uses early, calculate only what they require and keep the approval trail clear enough for the next revision.
