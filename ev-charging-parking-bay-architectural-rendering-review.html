How to review EV charging bays in architectural renderings.

Charging equipment changes more than a parking-space label. Use this source-led visual checklist to coordinate chargers, cable routes, bollards, pedestrian access and landscaping without suggesting technical certification.

What the render can and cannot establish

An electric-vehicle charging bay can look like an ordinary parking space with a small pedestal. That apparent simplicity is deceptive: the charger, parking geometry, pedestrian route, protection measures and cable reach may all affect the architecture. A rendering is useful for communicating their visual relationship, but it cannot establish electrical safety, charger capacity, fire strategy or accessibility compliance. The design team must approve those separately.

Define the image's purpose first: an early concept, a coordinated design review or a marketing illustration. A polished image should not imply that a provisional charging arrangement is installed or technically approved.

Ask for the right coordinated inputs

Request the latest site plan, parking layout, landscape plan, charger location schedule, electrical equipment assumptions, kerb and level details, lighting strategy and any applicable access or fire strategy drawings. Note each document's revision. Ask which equipment is selected and which is only a placeholder. If no charger model has been specified, use a visibly generic schematic object and record that limitation.

Separate decisions owned by the architect, electrical designer, parking consultant and landscape designer. The visualisation artist should not silently choose equipment specifications to complete the composition.

Check bay dimensions and the vehicle envelope

Compare the bay position, orientation and width against the issued parking plan, not against a convenient car model. Keep parking lines, kerbs, columns and wheel stops consistent between plan and perspective. Check that the rendered vehicle does not appear to overlap a walkway or block a neighbouring door. A wide-angle camera can make a tight bay look generous; retain a near-normal review view as well as the hero angle.

Use vehicle assets as scale references, never as evidence that manoeuvring clearance or access has been technically verified.

Model the charger and its protective elements

Check the pedestal or wall-box position, mounting height as shown in project documents, screen orientation, protective bollards, plinth and any adjacent cabinet. The equipment may require visible separation from landscaping, walls or vehicle paths. Do not remove a bollard because it interrupts a clean facade photograph. Do not invent a hidden electrical cabinet if the equipment strategy remains undecided.

Where a specific manufacturer's product is actually selected, verify its external dimensions against current manufacturer information supplied by the project team. Avoid copying product branding or claiming endorsement without permission.

Treat cable reach and routing as visible design questions

Charging sockets sit in different positions on different vehicles. A single cable pose cannot prove universal reach. For the review image, show the intended charger location and an illustrative cable path without stretching it through the vehicle, across a planted bed or over a pedestrian route. Ask the electrical designer to confirm the intended cable-management solution and whether the chosen arrangement can serve the target bay.

In marketing images, it may be clearer to show the charger with its cable holstered. That choice should not conceal a known conflict in the design.

Protect the pedestrian route and accessible spaces

Trace the walking route from the charging bay to the building entrance. Compare level changes, kerbs, adjacent footways, door swings and potential cable crossings with the coordinated plans. Do not use people or parked vehicles to mask a narrow route. Accessible charging provisions vary by project and jurisdiction, so do not infer compliance from the presence of a wheelchair symbol or a generous-looking camera angle.

For the separate visual review of entrances, ramps and clear routes, see the accessible-route rendering checklist.

Keep landscape and drainage consistent

EV infrastructure often sits beside planting beds, permeable paving, rain gardens or a parking canopy. Verify the relationship between charger foundations, planting edges, drainage falls and the issued landscape geometry. A lush hedge should not cover the equipment access face, and a decorative planter should not replace a required protection element. Rainwater performance and electrical protection require specialist design; a visualisation only shows the agreed physical arrangement.

Review charging bays after dark

Evening imagery can reveal whether a charger is legible in the parking court, but its glow must not be used as a substitute for the actual lighting strategy. Check luminaire positions, parking markings and any intended equipment indicators against the brief. A bright animated screen or green halo can misrepresent the selected product. Keep the same equipment geometry and parking layout in both day and night versions.

Where external lighting affects neighbours, use the night-time light-spill review for visual coordination, not as a photometric certification.

Make markings and signage traceable

Charging-bay symbols, directional arrows, reserved-space markings and wayfinding signs should come from the approved signage or traffic plan. Do not add universal-looking symbols to imply that every parking bay includes charging. Check that signs remain visible when a car occupies the space, and that a rendered charging station is not shown in a different location from the site plan.

Where signage is provisional, identify it as illustrative rather than pretending a local marking standard has been confirmed.

Choose views that expose conflicts

Use three complementary views: a site-context image showing the bay's relationship to circulation; a pedestrian-height view showing the charger, kerb and walking route; and a closer coordination view of the equipment and any protective bollards. Keep camera IDs and model revisions. If the marketing camera hides the charger behind a car, the coordination view must still show it clearly.

Review the source plan and section beside the images. Perspective alone is a poor tool for judging exact distances.

Fictional example: courtyard parking with two chargers

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or an approved electrical design. Imagine a small apartment development with two charging bays beside a planted courtyard. The first hero render places a charger against the boundary hedge, omits the protective post and shows a cable crossing the pedestrian path. The team flags these issues and requests coordinated input before preparing a revised image.

Record approval and publication limits

For each final view, retain the site-plan revision, equipment assumptions, camera ID, model version and list of unresolved questions. Ask the architect to approve the spatial depiction, the electrical designer to confirm equipment assumptions, and relevant specialists to verify access, safety and servicing requirements. If a charger changes location or type, review every affected image rather than retouching only the close-up.

Use an explicit note such as “illustrative charging equipment, subject to detailed design” when that is true. Do not use a disclaimer to excuse a known contradiction with issued drawings. For managing image changes, see the rendering revision checklist.

Pre-publication visual checklist

  • Parking bay and charger positions match the issued layout.
  • Pedestals, cabinets, bollards and foundations reflect documented decisions.
  • Vehicles, cables and kerbs do not conceal known route conflicts.
  • Accessible routes and any reserved bays are depicted without implied certification.
  • Landscape, paving, drainage edges and lighting remain coordinated.
  • Day and night views use consistent equipment and signage.
  • Drawing revisions, provisional assumptions and approval owners are recorded.
  • Marketing images do not claim charging availability, capacity or safety without evidence.
