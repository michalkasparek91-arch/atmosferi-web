How to review construction hoarding in architectural renderings

A beautiful completed-street image can conceal months of temporary fencing and access changes. This guide helps architecture teams depict the right construction milestone without confusing a visual check with a site-safety approval.

Decide what the image promises

A final-vision rendering and a first-occupation rendering answer different questions. A temporary construction boundary may be irrelevant to the former but essential to the latter. Label the milestone before modelling anything, and never imply that a construction phase is already complete.

Start with a dated logistics and phasing set

Collect the current construction logistics plan, hoarding layout, site boundary plan, pedestrian management drawings, gate schedule and relevant planning conditions. Identify which drawings are approved and which remain provisional. An artist cannot establish a safe pedestrian diversion from a marketing masterplan alone.

Trace the hoarding line from the plan

Place the hoarding along the documented boundary rather than following a convenient pavement joint. Check offsets around entrances, corners, utilities and neighbouring property. If two source plans disagree, flag the discrepancy instead of quietly choosing the line that makes the image more attractive.

Show realistic panel height and ground interfaces

Use specified panel dimensions and finish. On sloping ground, confirm whether the panels step, follow the grade or sit above a retaining element. Do not stretch a generic texture to hide the difference between the top edge and actual ground levels.

Keep construction gates and swing areas visible

Mark delivery gates, pedestrian gates and any controlled access points from the current schedule. A closed-gate image must not suggest that vehicles pass through a solid panel. For an open-gate view, show the leaf and its occupied space where relevant. Refer manoeuvring questions to the logistics designer.

Protect the truth of the pedestrian route

A narrow strip of pavement can look generous in a wide-angle image. Compare the temporary footway, barriers, crossing points and accessible diversions with issued drawings. Do not remove ramps, tactile features or protective barriers to create a cleaner foreground. A render is not proof of route accessibility.

Separate public access from site deliveries

Check the location of delivery approaches and the relationship between site gates and public circulation. Avoid showing a loading vehicle parked across a pedestrian diversion unless that exact managed scenario is intentional and documented. For vehicle movement geometry, see the delivery-access visual review.

Treat graphics and wayfinding as approved content

Hoarding graphics can include marketing images, directions, project names or legally required notices. Use supplied artwork and permissions. Do not invent safety signs, logos, contact numbers, planning approval claims or promotional promises. If the graphics are undecided, label the panels as placeholders in the review export.

Include the context that makes the boundary credible

Existing shopfronts, adjacent entrances, bus stops, trees, lighting and utilities often explain why a hoarding line bends. Keep these visible where they affect public understanding. Check that the CGI does not imply closure of a neighbouring entrance or removal of a protected tree without a documented proposal.

Test the scheme at eye level and in overview

An overview establishes the full boundary; an eye-level view tests what a person sees approaching the site. Use the same milestone and geometry in both. Crop the eye-level image at intended publication size, especially on a phone, to ensure temporary route arrows and gate locations are not visually lost.

Fictional example: a mixed-use infill site

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A proposed infill building is marketed before construction finishes. The first-occupation view shows a completed plaza, although the adjacent plot remains behind temporary hoarding. The architect supplies a dated phase plan. The revised first-occupation image restores the hoarding, relocates a temporary wayfinding sign to its documented position and adds a phase caption. A separate final-vision image retains the completed plaza with an explicit future-state label.

Record assumptions and changes

Log the phase date, drawing revisions, panel finish, gate state, camera IDs and unresolved signage approvals. Name files by phase so an early-occupation image cannot accidentally replace the final-vision export. Use the post-publication change-control checklist if the logistics plan changes after release.

Pre-publication hoarding review checklist

  • The depicted milestone and source drawing dates are recorded.
  • Hoarding line, height, ground transitions and gates match supplied plans.
  • Public routes and temporary barriers remain visible.
  • Adjacent entrances and street furniture are not misleadingly erased.
  • Graphics and logos have a confirmed source or are marked placeholders.
  • Eye-level and overview images show the same construction state.
  • Safety, accessibility and construction logistics approval are not claimed by the render.
  • Captions distinguish first occupation from final vision.

Show the right phase, not just the cleanest picture.
