How to review rammed-earth walls in architectural renderings

A practical visual QA workflow for stratification, texture scale, reveals and material uncertainty before approving a CGI image.

Why rammed earth needs a different visual review

A rammed-earth wall is not simply a beige plaster surface with horizontal noise. Its appearance is shaped by the formwork, compaction lifts, aggregate mix, construction method and exposure. A beautiful render can imply a construction detail that has never been resolved. This guide helps architects and visualisation teams check what the image communicates before using it for client approval or marketing. It is a visual coordination checklist, not structural, moisture, fire or material-performance advice. The project architect and specialist must resolve those questions.

Build a source register before building a material

Request the current wall elevations, sections, opening details, structural concept, finish references and any sample-panel photographs that the team is permitted to use. Record which images are design references rather than specifications. Ask whether the proposal uses unstabilised earth, stabilised rammed earth, a prefabricated panel, or an earth-like finish; these are not interchangeable visual claims. Record wall thickness, intended lift height, aggregate colour range, formwork joints and surface treatment only where actually documented. An attractive reference image from a different project is not permission to copy its exact stratification.

Represent compaction lifts without turning them into stripes

Compaction can leave horizontal bands, but their visibility and regularity depend on the mix and process. Avoid evenly spaced, high-contrast lines copied across every wall. Use a measured elevation and sample-panel evidence to establish a plausible scale. Check that a close crop shows subtle variation within a lift and that a distant crop reads as a wall rather than a stack of thin slabs. If the construction sequence is undecided, describe the pattern as illustrative rather than implying a fixed lift schedule.

Verify texture scale against openings and known dimensions

Compare visible grain and lift rhythm with a door height, window reveal and a dimensioned wall section. A procedural shader may look convincing at one camera distance while producing oversized stones in another. Lock the physical material scale across all views; do not resize a UV texture separately on each elevation to improve composition. Review a front-on crop and a grazing-angle crop at the final image resolution. Record the material version so a later camera change does not quietly change the design.

Check corners, reveals, wall heads and junctions

A flat material can conceal the most important design questions. At external corners, inspect whether the intended lift pattern wraps, terminates or changes with a construction joint. Around windows and doors, show the actual reveal depth and any documented lintel, frame or protection detail. Do not invent a seamless earth lintel or exposed unprotected wall head. Coordinate the visible edge condition with the architect's current detail package. The window reveal review provides a separate check for geometry and depth.

Keep moisture and weathering assumptions explicit

Surface colour may change under rain, shade and varying moisture conditions. A dramatic streak can suggest a defect, while a perfectly uniform finish may overstate consistency. Unless there is an approved weathering reference, use restrained variation and mark any patina as illustrative. Confirm whether a plinth, roof overhang, cap or other protection is shown in the design documents. Do not treat a rendered protective detail as proof that the assembly will be durable in a given climate.

Compare neutral daylight with the hero lighting

Low sun can exaggerate every horizontal line and make the material appear deeply grooved. Diffuse light can hide the same relief. Keep one neutral, colour-managed crop alongside the preferred hero image. Compare wall colour, relief, shadowing and roughness at the same exposure. Avoid increasing displacement until the surface resembles carved stone; physical relief should follow the reference sample and camera distance. For colour sign-off, follow the screen colour approval workflow.

Detect obvious tiling, mirrored patches and image artefacts

Large unbroken elevations make repeated material textures especially visible. Review the wall at full export size, at website width and in a small thumbnail. Look for identical knots, mirrored streaks, horizontal seams and unrealistic colour gradients. Variation should be anchored to the material logic rather than random patches. In animation, inspect several adjacent frames for flickering displacement or texture detail. If you simplify a distant material for stability, retain the approved wall geometry and a comparison crop.

Separate visual errors from unresolved design decisions

Use a short issue log with wall ID, camera ID, source drawing revision, reference sample, observed mismatch, decision owner and status. Categorise each item as a rendering correction, design question or illustrative assumption. A missing window head detail is not a Photoshop retouching request. Keep provisional items visible in the approval record and confirm whether they may appear in published marketing. For the wider sign-off process, use the material finish approval checklist.

Fictional example: a small rammed-earth visitor centre

Fictional illustrative scenario, not a commissioned or built Atmosferi project. A proposed visitor centre has one long earth wall and three recessed windows. The first render uses a strongly banded procedural texture; the stripes repeat every metre and continue through the window voids. The architect supplies a concept section and photographs of an approved test panel. The visualiser calibrates texture scale to the section, checks the corner and reveals, and prepares a neutral daylight crop beside the hero image. The wall-head protection is still unresolved, so the team records it as a design decision rather than drawing a fictional construction solution.

Pre-publication review checklist

  • The wall system is identified correctly, or explicitly described as a concept.
  • Drawings, sample references and revisions are recorded.
  • Lift rhythm and grain scale are consistent across cameras.
  • Texture does not tile conspicuously or stretch at corners.
  • Openings, reveals and wall heads match documented geometry.
  • No unsupported lintel, waterproofing or structural detail is invented.
  • Weathering and colour are labelled illustrative where unapproved.
  • Neutral and dramatic lighting crops have been compared.
  • Web, thumbnail and animation exports have been checked for artefacts.
  • Unresolved material decisions have a named reviewer and status.
  • The image makes no unverified performance or durability claim.

Make the material decision visible.
