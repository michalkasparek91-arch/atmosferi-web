How to review roof lightning protection in architectural renderings

Small air terminals and conductor routes can disappear from polished roof images. Review them against the supplied drawings without turning a visual into a claim of electrical safety.

Why lightning protection deserves a separate visual check

Lightning protection can be nearly invisible in a street-level hero view and surprisingly prominent from a neighbouring building. Air terminals, roof conductors, clips, supports and down-conductor routes can affect the perceived roof edge, metalwork and equipment layout. Their omission may create a misleadingly clean image. This is a guide to accurate representation of supplied design information, not to designing a lightning protection system or verifying compliance. The responsible specialist must establish whether a system is required and what it contains.

Ask for a coordinated protection layout

Request the latest roof plan, lightning protection layout, relevant sections, elevations, roof equipment plan and the design-stage register. Check the drawing revision and whether the layout is approved, preliminary or still being coordinated. An electrical consultant's schematic may locate equipment without giving exact support heights. Ask for supplier references or a dimensioned detail before building precise-looking components. If no layout exists, do not decorate the roof with generic rods and describe them as a designed solution.

Distinguish air terminals from other roof objects

An air terminal is not a vent pipe, antenna, handrail post or roof anchor. In a high camera view, those objects can have similar silhouettes. Keep each on the correct layer and tag it in the review model. The shape, location and height of a terminal must follow supplied documents. Avoid borrowing an impressive product photograph as a substitute for a specified assembly. A technical legend or a small annotated crop can help reviewers identify ambiguous elements.

Trace conductors rather than drawing arbitrary lines

Conductors may cross flat roofs, follow perimeter routes or connect with other documented components. Their visible path, supports and junctions depend on the project. Check changes in roof level, parapet corners and equipment zones against the coordinated plan. A conductor should not pass through a rooflight or vanish behind a planter merely to preserve composition. Where the routing is not final, represent the agreed provisional corridor and flag it as unresolved.

Review parapets, flashings and roof edges

The roof edge is where small details can become visually consequential. A conductor or terminal may be seen above the parapet in a raised camera view even if hidden from the street. Compare the parapet section, coping geometry and any specified fixing arrangement. Do not invent a waterproofing penetration or assume that a conductor can be embedded in a finish without approval. A photorealistic joint is not evidence that its weathering or electrical continuity has been resolved.

Coordinate with PV arrays and rooftop plant

Solar modules, mechanical units, access hatches, rooflights and plant screens occupy the same roofscape. Compare their latest plans rather than modelling each trade in isolation. A lightning protection element may be obscured by a PV row in one camera yet visible in another. Do not imply that an attractive separation distance is technically sufficient; the specialist must determine any required clearances and bonding strategy. The PV array review addresses module geometry, not lightning system design.

Do not lose down-conductor routes in facade retouching

Where the project documents show visible down-conductors, check elevations and facade junctions. Their alignment may interact with cladding joints, rainwater pipes, window reveals and signage. A thin conductor can disappear when the image is downsized or retouched. If the design uses concealed routing, do not add exposed strips for dramatic effect. The distinction should come from the drawings, not an aesthetic preference in Photoshop.

Use complementary cameras to catch omissions

A street-level camera tests whether terminals affect the silhouette; an elevated oblique view reveals roof routes and equipment conflicts; a close detail crop tests scale and support placement. Review all three at the intended export resolution. If the final marketing image is too distant to resolve the conductor, retain the higher-resolution coordination crop in the approval package. Visibility in a rendering is a camera-dependent observation, not proof of the real-world system's effectiveness.

Avoid overstating metallic finish and reflections

Tiny metallic components can become bright visual accents under strong HDRI lighting. Use the supplied finish reference and plausible roughness rather than making every terminal polished chrome. Verify whether conductors and supports should be visibly distinct from adjacent roof coverings. A neutral daytime light pass often reveals missing connections better than sunset lighting. Keep artistic grading separate from a technical review export so dark areas do not conceal unresolved elements.

Separate concept assumptions from issued design

At concept stage the project may show only a reserved strategy. A planning image may need a restrained representation with a documented note. Later tender or marketing imagery should reflect the latest coordinated issue, with changes tracked. Never quietly carry a guessed early layout into a supposedly final image. Mark a component as provisional in the review record rather than printing a claim of completed engineering on the public-facing visual.

Fictional example: a mixed-use roof with solar panels

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A compact mixed-use building has two rooflights, a PV array and a rooftop ventilation unit. The initial visual model shows a smooth parapet and no lightning protection elements because the electrical layout was not supplied. The architect later provides a preliminary routing diagram and a terminal schedule. The visualiser adds only the documented visible items, creates a raised roof review view and flags two junctions beside the PV array for specialist coordination. The images do not claim a compliant or installed system.

Make the approval record useful after revisions

Keep a concise log with the drawing references, model version, camera IDs, component visibility, known assumptions and named technical decision owners. Separate a visual correction, such as a missing terminal, from an engineering question, such as a proposed conductor route. When roof equipment moves, check every affected viewpoint. The rendering change-control guide explains how to manage updates to previously delivered images.

Roof lightning protection rendering checklist

  • The specialist's latest layout and revision are recorded.
  • Air terminals are not confused with vents or safety anchors.
  • Visible conductor routes follow documented geometry.
  • Parapet and coping interfaces match approved details where available.
  • PV modules, rooflights and plant are coordinated visually.
  • Down-conductor visibility follows the elevation drawings.
  • Street, elevated and detail views are checked at export size.
  • Unresolved fixings and routes are labelled provisional.
  • No safety, electrical or code compliance is inferred from the render.

Visual sign-off confirms representation against the supplied information. Design, installation and verification remain the responsibility of qualified project specialists.
