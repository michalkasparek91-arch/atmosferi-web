How to review exposed CLT panel joints in interior renderings

A drawing-led visual quality check for exposed cross-laminated timber surfaces, where panelisation and coordination matter more than a convincing wood texture.

Start with the panel schedule, not a timber shader

Exposed cross-laminated timber (CLT) is both a visible surface and part of a designed building assembly. A polished wood material can disguise incorrect panel sizes, seam locations and service penetrations. The first review question is not whether the grain looks attractive; it is whether the rendered surface matches the current structural and architectural information.

Request the current structural panel layout or fabrication assumptions, reflected ceiling plans, interior elevations and finish specification. Record the revision and identify which surfaces will remain exposed. If panelisation has not been coordinated, label the rendered seam layout as illustrative and subject to design confirmation, rather than silently presenting it as a construction decision.

Map panel boundaries across walls and ceilings

Draw a simple panel-boundary overlay on the camera view. Check whether visible seams align with the proposed panel dimensions and the structural grid, and whether a ceiling seam continues logically into an adjacent bay. Do not invent a uniform decorative board rhythm. CLT panels are not narrow timber cladding boards; their face appearance and joints follow a different manufacturing and assembly logic.

At corners, determine whether a panel end, return, cover strip or other designed junction should be visible. A seam that disappears behind furniture in one image may become a glaring discontinuity in another. Use the same source schedule across all approved cameras.

Separate designed gaps from rendering artefacts

At a typical viewing distance, panel joints may be subtle. A deep black line on every seam can falsely imply open gaps, while a seamless surface can erase the intended panel module. Ask the design team what joint treatment is specified: expressed gap, tight joint, sealant, trim or a detail still under review. Avoid assuming that a visible shadow line has a particular fire, acoustic or movement performance.

Test one neutral crop at an oblique angle and one front-facing crop. Check joint width in pixels against the known panel scale, then compare the two crops. If the source detail is unresolved, document the visual placeholder separately from technical approval.

Keep face grain plausible without repeating a texture tile

A single wood bitmap repeated over every panel produces identical knots and colour changes, particularly obvious on broad ceilings. Use reference material from the selected appearance grade when available, and vary the texture mapping without making each panel a random species or colour. Confirm grain direction from the intended exposed face and do not infer internal layer orientation from the surface image alone.

Check that UV mapping does not stretch around openings or rotate unexpectedly on returns. For large surfaces, a neutral orthographic or near-orthographic review crop can reveal repetition that cinematic perspective conceals.

Review openings, cut edges and reveals

Doors, windows, stairs and service openings expose the weaknesses of an otherwise convincing material. Verify opening dimensions from the latest drawings, the thickness visible at reveals, and whether the edge is meant to show timber, receive a lining or be concealed by another finish. Do not depict a raw exposed edge merely because the 3D model contains a cut.

Where a steel connection, bearing detail or interface with another material is not designed, keep the geometry provisional. A visualisation can show a plausible architectural intent, but it cannot establish structural adequacy or fire protection.

Coordinate lights, sprinklers and ventilation with the visible panel grid

Exposed ceilings make every downlight, detector, diffuser, sprinkler and cable route more noticeable. Overlay the current reflected ceiling plan and building-services layout. Check that fixtures do not accidentally intersect panel seams, beams or proposed access zones. Recessed fittings may require a specific construction detail; the renderer should not imply that arbitrary recesses can be cut into a structural panel.

Use the ventilation diffuser review and sprinkler coordination guide for their separate visual checks. Any technical clearance or penetration question goes back to the responsible consultant.

Test finish and lighting without inventing product approval

Distinguish the specified finish from the lighting effect. Warm evening light can make a neutral timber sample appear orange; a strong colour grade can hide uneven panel tone. Review a daylight crop under controlled white balance and then the intended hero lighting. Record whether the material comes from a physical sample, supplier reference or temporary visual placeholder.

Surface gloss, visible grain and colour should be reviewed together, but no rendering alone proves a coating's durability, cleanability or fire classification. Those claims belong to verified product documentation and the project team.

Check junctions with partitions, floors and acoustic treatments

Where partitions meet an exposed CLT soffit, verify whether the junction is expressed, sealed, concealed or still unresolved. The same applies at the wall base, suspended acoustic elements, curtain pockets and glazing heads. A beautiful uninterrupted timber ceiling may conflict with a later acoustic strategy, so do not omit visible treatments merely to improve the image.

Cross-reference the acoustic panel layout guide and the floor transition review where those elements enter the frame. Keep architectural appearance and compliance decisions in separate approval columns.

Make the review reproducible across versions

For every comparison, lock camera, focal length, sun position, exposure and colour treatment. Show a full room view for context plus crops at a representative seam, an opening and a service cluster. Annotate each crop with its drawing reference and the issue to resolve. A before-and-after image is useful only when the changed variable is clear.

Record decisions as verified from source, illustrative pending confirmation or requires consultant decision. This avoids treating a renderer's temporary workaround as a signed-off design detail. The visualisation uncertainty register provides a broader handover structure.

Fictional example: a small timber learning centre

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or built project. A proposed learning centre has an exposed CLT ceiling above a public lobby. An early CGI uses one continuous wood texture and evenly spaced black grooves. The design team supplies a preliminary panel plan showing larger modules, and the services engineer marks several ceiling devices near one joint.

The next review uses the same camera and daylight. One annotated image corrects the panel boundaries and reduces exaggerated joint contrast. A second highlights the unresolved service positions. The image is not described as a fabrication model, fire strategy or acoustic assessment. The outcome is a clear list of visual corrections and design questions, not a claim that the building has been technically approved.

Pre-publication CLT visual QA checklist

  • Current structural and architectural drawing revisions are recorded.
  • Exposed versus concealed timber surfaces are confirmed.
  • Panel module and joint layout follow available design information.
  • Joint width, depth and finish are verified or explicitly provisional.
  • Grain direction, texture scale and repeated knots are checked.
  • Opening edges, returns and panel thickness do not contradict details.
  • Lighting, diffusers, sprinklers and other services are coordinated visually.
  • Partitions, acoustic elements and floor junctions remain visible where designed.
  • Neutral-light and hero-light crops use traceable settings.
  • Unresolved fire, structural and acoustic matters are referred to qualified consultants.
  • Final images identify their drawing revisions and approvers.

A rendering is a communication and review tool, not a substitute for engineering, product certification or construction documentation.
