How to review raised access floor panels in interior renderings

A practical visual QA guide for raised access floor panel grids, perimeter cuts, thresholds, access lids and furniture coordination in office renderings.

What the rendering must prove

A raised access floor is not merely a decorative square grid. It is an assembly of panels, supports and interfaces that influences visible joint positions, thresholds, furniture and service access. A compelling office image can conceal a grid that conflicts with the current setting-out plan. This guide is a visual coordination checklist, not an assessment of floor load capacity, fire performance or installation compliance.

Start with the architectural floor setting-out drawing, current furniture plan, floor finish schedule and relevant services coordination plans. Note revision numbers. Identify which elements are approved, which are provisional and which are outside the camera. Never infer the actual support system from a texture alone.

Use the actual panel module and datum

Confirm the proposed module and the origin of the setting-out grid. A repeating 600 mm texture, for example, is not proof that the selected floor system uses that dimension. Use the actual manufacturer's and design team's information rather than assuming a standard. Align panel joints to the agreed datum, not the centre of the hero camera.

Overlay the panel boundaries on the floor plan, then compare a top-down model export with that plan. If a workstation island or partition has shifted, check whether the floor grid also changed. Keep grid geometry consistent across all views.

Inspect perimeter cuts and wall interfaces

Uniform full panels rarely reach every wall without cuts. Check how the proposed system meets curved walls, angled glazing, columns and door frames. An image that runs perfect squares straight through a column base or facade mullion may look tidy but communicate an impossible layout.

Show perimeter pieces only to the level of detail supported by the drawings. Do not invent minimum cut sizes, support spacing or edge restraints. Those decisions belong to the floor-system designer and project team.

Distinguish structural panel joints from finish joints

Carpet tiles, timber finishes, resilient flooring and stone may have their own module or continuous surface treatment above the raised floor. A visible 600 mm grid in the final image is wrong if the selected finish conceals the access-panel seams. Conversely, a removable finish-infill access panel may need a visible edge.

Compare the structural access-floor setting-out with the finish schedule. Record which seams should be visible, subdued or hidden. For floor material changes, see the floor finish transition guide.

Check door thresholds and level changes

A raised floor meets doorways, lift lobbies, stairs, ramps and adjoining slab levels. In a visualisation, the critical question is whether the proposed finished floor levels and threshold profiles are represented consistently. A continuous-looking floor across an unresolved level difference can imply a decision that nobody has approved.

Compare section details and finished-floor-level annotations. Mark any unconfirmed transition as provisional. The rendering is not evidence that an accessible route, fire compartmentation or other regulatory requirement has been satisfied.

Keep access to service zones credible

Raised floors are often chosen for access to power, data or other distributed services. A beautiful carpet pattern should not erase access lids, grilles or floor boxes where the coordinated drawings require them. Equally, adding generic square hatches at every workstation is not a valid substitute for a services plan.

Cross-check the office floor-box guide for device placement and furniture conflicts. Do not illustrate cable routes or penetrations that have not been coordinated by the relevant designers.

Test furniture and partition interfaces

A floor access point can become unreachable under a fixed bench, joinery unit or meeting-table base. Partitions may also require specific support or fixing details that a rendering cannot invent. Review the visible arrangement at both occupied and unoccupied furniture positions.

Use a plan crop to mark floor-box positions, panel seams and fixed furniture footprints. Where a conflict exists, request a design decision instead of moving the hatch to a convenient spot in the 3D scene.

Review at three scales

An eye-level marketing view is useful for atmosphere but often hides the floor. Use a near-orthographic plan or overhead view to verify module and setting-out, a medium perspective to check perimeter pieces and thresholds, and a close crop to assess visible joint and lid treatment.

Lock camera, lighting and colour settings between revision comparisons. Avoid judging a small joint from a heavily compressed preview. Store each review image with its drawing revision and an issue identifier.

Separate modelling errors from unresolved design

If a drawing clearly specifies a joint and the model omits it, that is a visual correction. If the drawings disagree about a threshold, floor-box position or finish treatment, the design team must resolve it first. Do not let a rendering artist's aesthetic preference become a hidden specification.

Use three labels in the review pack: verified against current drawings, illustrative pending confirmation, and consultant decision required. The visualisation uncertainty register explains how to hand over these open questions.

Fictional example: an office refurbishment

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi client project or built case study. An office refurbishment has a proposed raised access floor beneath carpet tiles. The first CGI shows prominent square seams across the whole room. The finish schedule, however, calls for a continuous-looking carpet tile surface, while the services plan identifies two specific floor-box covers.

The review separates the concealed structural panel grid from the visible carpet pattern, adds only the coordinated covers and flags a threshold detail awaiting approval. The revised image represents the intended appearance without claiming that the support system or services installation has been technically verified.

Pre-publication visual QA checklist

Confirm the floor-system and finish drawings are current; record grid origin and module; inspect perimeter cuts and columns; distinguish visible finish joints from hidden panel seams; compare thresholds with finished-floor levels; show approved access covers and floor boxes; test fixed furniture conflicts; use overhead, perspective and detail crops; record all unresolved decisions; and recheck every affected image after changes.

Approval of a rendering means approval of what is shown at a documented design revision. It does not replace supplier information, structural calculations, accessibility assessment, fire design or site coordination.

Review floor setting-out before final export

Share the current floor plan, finishes, services layout and key camera views so that visual corrections and unresolved design decisions remain distinct.
