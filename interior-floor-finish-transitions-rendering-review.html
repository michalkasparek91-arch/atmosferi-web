Floor finish transitions: an interior rendering review

Before approving a polished interior image, check the often-overlooked line where two floor finishes meet. This practical review keeps thresholds, materials and levels faithful to the design.

Why floor transitions need a separate visual check

A floor can look seamless in a beauty image even when the specification calls for different finishes, levels or transition profiles. These junctions matter at doorways, wet areas, circulation routes and changes from tile to timber or carpet. This guide is about visual fidelity to coordinated design information, not installation instructions or an accessibility certificate.

The useful question is not whether the rendered floor looks tidy. It is whether a viewer could reasonably mistake a proposed material boundary, level change or threshold for a different design decision.

Build a source set before drawing a neat line

Request the current floor-finish plan and legend, room data or finish schedule, door schedule, relevant sections, accessibility strategy where applicable, and supplier details for specified profiles. Check revisions against the model and the furniture plan. A mood-board image is insufficient to establish a junction location or height.

Make a register of each visible transition: room or door ID, finish on either side, nominal level relationship, specified trim if any, source revision and decision owner. Where the documents disagree, ask the architect rather than selecting the least visible option.

Place the material boundary where the design puts it

At an internal doorway, the transition may be aligned with a closed door leaf, a frame line or another documented setting-out reference. It should not be moved simply to make the hero camera cleaner. Compare plan geometry against the door frame and wall thickness, especially where the door is open in the image.

In open-plan rooms, a change from kitchen tile to living-room timber can follow a deliberate grid or edge. Verify the boundary from the finish plan rather than inventing a straight line under the island. If the transition is unresolved, identify it as provisional in the review sheet.

Separate a finish change from a level change

Different finish build-ups do not automatically mean that a visible step exists. Conversely, a model that flattens two floor levels can conceal a real threshold. Read sections and finished-floor-level notes before adjusting geometry. Do not infer finished heights from the apparent thickness of a material swatch.

A narrow dark strip is not a substitute for modelling the specified edge. For any visible upstand, ramp or change in level, confirm which specialist owns the design and whether the approved drawings actually show it.

Show specified transition profiles without inventing them

A flush joint, cover strip, edge trim and movement profile have different visible geometries. Use the manufacturer's or designer's information when available, including material and finish. Avoid a universal shiny metal strip between every pair of finishes; it is a common rendering shortcut that can imply an unapproved product.

At a close camera angle, check the width, reflectance and shadow of the profile. In a wide shot, preserve its location even when it occupies only a few pixels. If no profile is specified, do not fabricate a technical detail to fill the gap.

Coordinate the threshold with doors and skirtings

An open door can conceal a transition placed on the wrong side of the leaf. Check frame depth, swing, leaf position, floor edge and skirting returns in the same model revision. Sliding doors, pocket doors and full-height frames each expose different parts of the junction.

Use the interior door and hardware review for the separate question of operation and hardware. Here the focus is the material and level interface beneath the opening.

Treat wet-room edges as a coordination question

Bathrooms, utility rooms and entrances may include falls, water-management details or different floor assemblies. The renderer should depict only the visible features supported by the approved documentation. Do not invent a raised threshold as proof of water containment or erase a documented change of level to imply barrier-free access.

A beautiful shower image cannot verify waterproofing or slip resistance. If a floor fall or drain arrangement affects the visible surface, refer it to the responsible designer and record the source. For a broader check of the room layout, see the restroom visual review.

Carry the laying pattern through the boundary

Material scale, tile grid, timber board direction and carpet texture should remain consistent with the specified finish. A texture seam can accidentally suggest an extra joint or a change in board width. Check the transition from a plan view as well as from the intended camera.

Where two patterned finishes meet, review cut pieces, alignment and the effect of perspective. Do not silently rotate boards or stretch tiles to eliminate awkward cuts if the laying direction is already defined. Mark unresolved setting-out as illustrative.

Avoid visual claims about accessibility

A visually flush junction is not evidence that the completed building meets accessibility requirements. Small changes in level, bevels and surface properties can matter but cannot be validated from a marketing image. Refer threshold geometry and accessible-route decisions to the project team and applicable requirements.

For the wider route, turning spaces and external-to-internal continuity, consult the accessible-route accuracy guide. This article addresses the local finish junction and how it is represented in CGI.

Use lighting to reveal rather than disguise the junction

Grazing light can make a tiny trim appear oversized, while broad diffuse lighting can erase a real step. Review the junction under the planned lighting setup and one neutral inspection view. Check that reflections on metal, tile and varnished timber remain plausible and do not turn the profile into a bright decorative stripe.

Keep the same material definitions across close-ups, panoramas and animation frames. A transition that vanishes between views may indicate a model inconsistency rather than a creative lighting choice.

Fictional example: an apartment entrance

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission or an approved detail. A proposed apartment has porcelain tile in the entrance and engineered timber in the living room. The first rendering blends the two textures beneath an open door, although the floor-finish plan places the boundary at the frame line and the door schedule shows a different leaf position.

The visualiser corrects the boundary using the latest drawings, checks whether a trim has actually been specified, and creates a neutral close-up for the architect. The review log states “profile unresolved; visual representation provisional”. No installation, accessibility or durability claim is made.

Approve a junction with evidence, not just a hero image

For each critical location, capture a plan crop, one perspective close-up and a note naming the governing drawing revision. Record finish codes, boundary location, level status, profile status and unresolved questions. Ask the architect to approve the visual depiction, while technical performance and construction decisions remain with the appropriate designers.

When a finish schedule changes, update all affected views and cropped marketing assets. An apparently minor threshold revision can otherwise leave the brochure, website and animation showing different versions of the same apartment.

Pre-release floor transition checklist

Check that every visible finish boundary matches the latest plan; the finishes and laying directions agree with the schedule; levels are not flattened or invented; transition profiles have a documented basis; door leaves, frames and skirtings meet the floor coherently; wet-area details are not represented as performance proof; and provisional decisions are explicitly logged.

A final visual sign-off should identify what was checked and what remains outside the rendering team's scope. That makes the image useful for design communication without pretending that pixels can certify construction.
