How to write an architecture studio services page that helps clients choose

A services page should connect what the practice actually offers to a prospective client's project, not leave the visitor decoding a list of impressive-sounding disciplines.

A services page should help someone recognise a fit

An architecture practice's services page has a different job from its portfolio. The portfolio shows what has been designed; the services page tells a prospective client whether the practice can help with their situation, at the stage they are in. A single poetic paragraph about place and material is rarely enough to answer that question.

Write for a person making an initial decision, not for an awards jury. They may know the desired outcome but not the names of architectural work stages. A good page makes the types of work, intended clients, typical collaboration and next step easy to understand without promising services the practice does not actually offer.

Separate the audience from the project type

Consider who is buying the work and why. Homeowners may need help understanding an extension, renovation or new home. Developers may need feasibility, design coordination or residential planning. Institutions may need specialist procurement or competition experience. Those groups do not all need a different website, but their priorities should be identifiable within the service descriptions.

Make the first screen answer three questions: what the practice does, whom it works with and where it can reasonably deliver. If the work is restricted to particular jurisdictions, say so. Remote design collaboration does not automatically confer permission or competence to sign local statutory submissions.

Describe each genuine service with the same useful pattern

A helpful service description starts with the client's problem, then gives a short account of the scope, a likely output and a relevant example. “Residential architecture” is a category label. “Feasibility and design for alterations to existing homes, from early layout options to coordinated documents where commissioned” tells the reader more.

Do not use an exhaustive list of technical activities simply because a competitor does. Include only work the practice can actually undertake. For unfamiliar terms, briefly explain the deliverable rather than hiding the important details behind acronyms.

Clarify the stages without inventing a universal process

Clients may assume that hiring an architect always includes everything from first sketch to completed building. Explain the stages the studio can be appointed for, and where the scope changes. Early feasibility, concept design, planning submissions, technical documentation, tender support and site involvement are distinct tasks; the precise terminology and responsibility vary by country and contract.

State that the actual appointment, approvals and specialist consultants are defined in the project agreement. A stage diagram can help, but avoid presenting it as a statutory pathway applicable everywhere. Where a practice only covers design, say so rather than letting polished construction photographs imply responsibility for delivery it did not have.

Connect each claim to the right project

Rather than placing a grid of unrelated images under every service, choose a small number of relevant projects that demonstrate that particular scope. For an adaptive reuse offer, a well-explained before-and-after project is more informative than a glossy unrelated villa. The link should open a case study that identifies the project's status and the studio's actual role.

For each service, one carefully chosen example may be enough. Avoid invented testimonials, arbitrary awards, stock imagery passed off as commissions and statements about measurable results without evidence. The architecture project case study guide shows how to describe a real design decision and distinguish proposals from completed work.

Make exclusions and specialist work understandable

If the practice offers architectural design but not engineering, interior fit-out, planning representation or on-site supervision as standard, make that clear before the first quote. A service page does not need a long legal disclaimer. It can simply explain that scope, specialist consultants and deliverables are confirmed in the appointment.

Keep distinct professional activities separate where different audiences or legal regimes apply. Independent expert reports, valuations and design commissions may require different evidence and contact flows. Avoid implying that specialist advice is part of an architectural package merely because the same studio or professional can provide it.

Choose one overview page or several focused pages deliberately

A small practice with three closely related offers can often explain them clearly on one services page. A studio serving materially different audiences may benefit from a concise overview linking to a few deeper pages with distinct information and examples. Create a separate page only when it genuinely answers a different question.

Do not manufacture a page for every city, synonym or stage just to capture search phrases. An architectural interior design page should explain different expertise from a residential feasibility page, not merely replace a headline. Link each relevant service to actual portfolio examples, the studio profile and the contact path. More pages are not automatically more useful.

Write for scanning while preserving architectural substance

Keep service names direct. Add a one-sentence explanation before any expressive studio language, then provide enough detail to resolve the most common uncertainty. Use a clear heading hierarchy and descriptive links. On phones, ensure the service list, supporting image and enquiry action remain in a sensible reading order.

Compare “Spatial transformation” with “Renovation and adaptive reuse”. The first may work as a creative tagline; the second helps a visitor understand the actual offer. This does not require flattening the studio's voice. It means placing the precise phrase where it makes the page useful.

Ask for only what helps the first conversation

An enquiry invitation should match the stage of the client. Someone exploring feasibility may have an address, photographs and an uncertain budget, not a full brief. Invite a short description, project location or region, intended timeline and any relevant drawings if they are available. Do not demand an upload of sensitive documents before the studio can explain how they will be handled.

Use one primary contact path and a visible fallback such as an email link. If the website has a form, test the actual delivery and failure states rather than assuming its success animation means the message was received. The contact form delivery guide addresses that technical handoff.

Keep the search signals tied to real page content

A good title may name the intended audience and service, such as “Residential architecture and renovation | Practice name”, where those are genuine offers. Give each distinct page a unique summary, one primary heading, meaningful visible copy and a self-referencing canonical when appropriate. Helpful alt text describes informative project imagery; it should not repeat the service keyword on every photograph.

Structured data should reflect visible, verifiable information. It will not make a weak page authoritative on its own. Avoid unsupported location claims, misleading project counts and large batches of near-duplicate local landing pages. Use a genuine services overview as the navigation hub, with deeper pages only where they add substance.

Fictional example: a practice with two complementary offers

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi client or a built commission. Imagine a small practice that undertakes residential alterations and early-stage feasibility studies for community buildings. Its old services page says only “Architecture, interiors and concepts” beneath several unlabeled images.

The revised page opens with a clear description of the two types of brief. One section explains when a homeowner should make contact and what the design-stage appointment may include. Another explains what a community organisation can bring to a feasibility conversation. Each section links to a clearly labelled hypothetical project example with an honest design status. The page closes with a simple enquiry route, not a claimed increase in lead volume. The separation helps clients understand fit without pretending the practice offers every possible service.

Pre-launch review with the project lead

Read the completed page as a prospective client unfamiliar with the studio. Can they identify a relevant service, its initial scope, the responsible discipline and a suitable example without guessing? Check that all illustrated work is credited and authorised, that the stated stages match actual contracts and that the copy does not promise regulated activities the practice cannot deliver in the target market.

Then test links, mobile readability, keyboard navigation, contact-form delivery and the page's relationship to the wider site. Review the copy when services or qualifications change.
