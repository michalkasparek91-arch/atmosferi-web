How to review curtain wall spandrel panels in architectural renderings

A source-led checklist for separating opaque spandrel zones from vision glazing without inventing a facade specification.

Why spandrel zones deserve their own review

A glazed facade can look continuous in a marketing image even when the construction drawings distinguish transparent vision glazing from opaque spandrel panels. Spandrels may conceal floor edges, insulation, back pans or other assemblies. Their appearance depends on the specified system, not simply a darker glass shader. The aim here is to keep the rendering faithful to the approved facade intent. This is a visual coordination method, not a thermal, fire or facade engineering assessment.

Start with the facade package, not a reference photograph

Request the latest elevations, curtain-wall setting-out drawings, typical vertical and horizontal sections, panel schedule, glazing specification and approved material or sample references. Confirm revision numbers and who owns each decision. Ask whether the spandrel is ceramic-fritted glass, an opaque coated glass assembly, metal infill or another specified system. Where the selection is unresolved, label it provisional. A photograph of a different building cannot establish the project's panel build-up or optical properties.

Create a panel-by-panel vision and spandrel map

Mark each elevation bay as vision, spandrel, opaque door panel or another defined category. Check floor-to-floor boundaries, parapets, corners, recessed bays and changes in facade module. A useful deliverable is a simple annotated elevation with panel IDs and status. Compare that map against the 3D model before adjusting any materials. This avoids a common failure: a persuasive full-height transparent facade that accidentally exposes slabs and interior spaces the design intends to conceal.

Align mullions, transoms and floor lines

Use actual curtain-wall grid dimensions where available. Verify that horizontal transoms, vertical mullions and opaque panels align with the issued elevation, including around corners and offsets. A spandrel band should not be moved up or down solely to make the image composition tidier. At close range, model the visible frame depth and panel setback; at distance, preserve the overall rhythm without inventing tiny seals or fixings.

Do not give opaque panels the behaviour of vision glass

Spandrel glass may reflect its surroundings, but the backing and build-up can make it appear different from transparent glazing. Avoid a single fully transparent material across the whole curtain wall. Equally, do not assume all opaque panels should be black or mirror-like. Compare a neutral daylight crop of one typical bay against the selected product information and any approved physical sample. Record colour and reflection decisions as visual approximations, not verified material performance.

Control what the viewer can see through the facade

Check whether furniture, lights, people and internal partitions are visible only behind vision glazing. The renderer may accidentally reveal a continuous office interior through an opaque floor-edge band. At night, bright interior illumination can make that mistake especially obvious. Use a paired daytime and dusk review where both views are in scope. Do not fabricate occupancy or interior fit-out details just to make the image livelier.

Review edges, corners and interfaces

Inspect transitions at slab edges, entrance canopies, opaque parapets, operable windows and adjoining solid cladding. A material assignment that works on the flat elevation may fail at a return, producing an unexplained transparent strip or a panel that ends mid-module. Request an enlarged crop for at least one typical corner and one atypical junction. If the detail is unresolved, flag it instead of drawing a construction solution in the rendering.

Test the same panel under different viewing conditions

Approve a neutral daylight camera before the hero image. Then compare a street-level oblique view, a frontal crop and, when relevant, a dusk view. The visual distinction between vision and spandrel areas may change with reflection angle. The goal is not to force a uniform contrast in every image; it is to prevent inconsistent materials, invented transparency or misleading tonal adjustments. Keep exposure and colour grading traceable between revisions.

Fictional example: an office building curtain wall

Fictional illustrative scenario, not an Atmosferi commission or verified built project. A proposed six-storey office facade has a regular glazed grid. The first visualisation applies one transparent material to every panel, making the slab edges visible. The architect supplies a marked-up elevation with opaque spandrel bands and a provisional glass finish. The visualiser updates the panel map, models the correct opaque zones and exports a neutral bay crop and street camera. The finish remains explicitly provisional pending sample approval; the render makes no claim about fire resistance or energy performance.

Keep a small approval and revision register

For each affected facade zone, record panel ID, source drawing, revision, system or finish status, reviewer, affected camera IDs and export version. Distinguish three things: an error in the render, a design decision still pending and a deliberate visual simplification. If the panel schedule changes after publication, update every affected image and crop. The rendering change-control guide describes how to manage that replacement.

Pre-publication spandrel visual QA checklist

  • Current facade elevations, sections and panel schedules are identified by revision.
  • Vision and opaque zones are mapped consistently across elevations.
  • Spandrel locations agree with floor edges and documented parapets.
  • Mullions, transoms, corners and panel setbacks follow the issued grid.
  • Opaque panels do not accidentally show interiors or structural slabs.
  • Colours and reflections follow approved references or are marked provisional.
  • Daylight and dusk crops do not contradict each other.
  • Unresolved junctions are flagged to the architect or facade consultant.
  • No fire, thermal, acoustic or structural performance is inferred from the render.
  • Review owner, camera IDs and final image revision are recorded.
