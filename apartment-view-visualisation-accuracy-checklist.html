How to verify the view from an unbuilt apartment.

A rendered outlook from an unbuilt apartment can be useful without pretending to be a photograph. This practical checklist shows how to document eye positions, window geometry, context and uncertainty before a unit-specific view is used in marketing.

By Michal Kašpárek, architect and founder of Atmosferi3 November 2026

Define the claim before choosing a camera.

A view from a proposed apartment is one of the most persuasive images in off-plan marketing. It is also unusually easy to overstate. A rendering can imply an uninterrupted skyline, a sea horizon or a landmark that a resident would only see while standing at a very specific point, or perhaps not at all once neighbouring construction is complete.

Start by deciding whether the deliverable is an interior atmosphere image, an indicative outward view or a survey-supported view study. Those are different products with different evidence requirements. A beautifully composed interior may legitimately prioritise mood, provided nobody mistakes the exterior for a verified outlook. A unit-specific view study needs a documented camera position and surrounding geometry.

Write down the exact question: “What would a standing person see through the living-room glazing of unit B-504 from the marked position?” is testable. “Show the spectacular view from the fifth floor” is not. Record which parts of the image are verified, modelled from plans or simply illustrative. This guide addresses the outward view from inside a proposed home, not the separate task of placing a proposed building into a street photograph.

Build a small evidence pack before modelling the skyline.

Ask the architect or developer for the latest approved unit plan, building section, floor-level schedule, facade drawings and a site model with a known coordinate reference. A site photograph without a recorded position is useful for context but weak as measurement. Request a topographic survey and relevant neighbouring-building information where the claimed view depends on them.

For each input, note its revision and date. Separate measured survey geometry from approximate massing, mapping data and future-development assumptions. If the neighbouring roof height comes from a simplified planning model rather than a measured survey, the final image should not silently promote that approximation to certainty.

The evidence pack should answer five questions: which unit, which window, which finished-floor level, which eye position and which surrounding obstructions. A missing answer is a reason to qualify the output, not a reason to improvise. The BIM and CAD handover guide covers model origins, file versions and coordinate checks in more detail.

Confirm the unit, level and vertical datum.

Floor labels such as “Level 5” are not elevations. A fifth-floor apartment can sit at different heights depending on the ground datum, floor-to-floor heights, podium and terrain. Obtain the finished floor level (FFL) in a declared datum and check it against the section. Then verify the exact unit location, orientation and glazing bay.

Do not mix survey elevation, model-local Z values and finished-floor levels without a documented transformation. If the site model uses a local zero and the architect's section uses a national height datum, record the offset and have the design team confirm it. An error of one storey can change the apparent skyline more than any lighting adjustment.

For developments with mirrored or repeated units, treat every different orientation or vertical position as a separate view configuration. A view approved for a west-facing fifth-floor living room is not automatically approved for the east-facing sixth-floor variant. Give each view a stable identifier tied to the unit schedule.

Specify the eye point rather than guessing a pleasing height.

Set the camera from a marked position on the unit plan. For a standing-eye illustration, a height such as 1.55 m above FFL can be a project assumption, not a universal standard. A seated-eye study needs a lower value chosen for the intended user and furniture layout. Record the actual height used, and never mix standing and seated views without labels.

The camera should not float beyond the glazing, inside a wall or above a balustrade simply to reveal a distant landmark. Measure the horizontal offset from the window, the camera heading and the intended field of view. A point at the centre of the room and a point immediately against the glass may produce very different visible angles, even though both are technically inside the same apartment.

When a brochure shows a panoramic composition, distinguish it from the narrower view through a single window. If several viewpoints are combined, label the image as a composite or illustrative panorama. A camera path or wide-angle sweep should not be described as one fixed human-eye view.

Make the glazing, frames and balustrades part of the view test.

The window opening is an optical constraint, not decoration. Model sill and head heights, mullions, balcony edges, deep reveals, structural columns and fixed screens from the current facade drawings. A one-metre camera shift can move a landmark behind a mullion. An omitted balcony slab can turn a narrow strip of sky into an apparent open horizon.

For a seated view, the sill may matter more than the skyline. For a standing view, the head height and balcony canopy can limit the vertical field. Show these elements in at least one validation frame, even if the final marketing image uses a softer interior treatment.

Transparent glazing does not make frames disappear. Avoid removing parapets or lowering balcony guards for composition. If reflections, tint or solar-control glass materially affect visibility, the atmospheric render can acknowledge them, while the geometry-check view remains legible and neutral. Never use a glassless opening to claim an unobstructed outlook that the built facade would not provide.

Test obstructions in three dimensions, not just on a map.

Use a coordinated 3D context model to check neighbouring facades, roof plant, parapets, terrain, retaining walls and nearby trees. A plan-only line toward a landmark ignores height. For a target point, trace the segment from the defined eye point to the target through the context model and identify any intersecting surfaces.

For a broad outlook, sample multiple directions rather than declaring the entire panorama visible because one line of sight clears a roof. Where context geometry is approximate, document the uncertainty. Existing trees may change the view seasonally; newly planted trees may grow into the sightline over time. The landscape accuracy guide explains how to distinguish current and proposed planting.

Also test the project itself. A wing, balcony partition or adjacent block within the same development may block a view before any off-site building does. Run the check against the coordinated current design, not an attractive early massing option that has since been superseded.

Separate what exists today from what may be built later.

Current photographs cannot prove a permanent future view. A cleared plot, an approved neighbouring project or a permitted roof extension may alter the outlook. Ask the project team which nearby developments should be represented and which planning sources are current. Record the information date and whether a future building is consented, proposed or merely a scenario.

Use separate labelled versions when necessary: “existing context as documented on [date]” and “illustrative context including identified proposals”. Do not blend uncertain future buildings into the same unqualified image, and do not erase an approved obstruction because it weakens the composition.

Planning data is jurisdiction-specific and can change. A visualisation studio should not promise to have exhaustively identified every possible future development. The responsible developer and its advisers should confirm the appropriate disclosure and verification process before any buyer-facing claim is published.

Choose a field of view that a reader can interpret.

A wide lens makes rooms appear generous and pushes distant buildings farther away; a narrow lens enlarges distant landmarks relative to the interior. Neither is automatically dishonest, but neither is a neutral representation of everything a person sees. Record the projection type, focal length or field-of-view angle, sensor or filmback settings and final crop.

A single rectilinear image cannot reproduce the full breadth of human vision without distortion or selective framing. For unit comparisons, use a consistent camera convention so one apartment is not rewarded with a telephoto landmark while another receives a wide interior shot. Consider pairing an appealing interior render with a plain, labelled view study from a fixed eye point.

Keep verticals and window geometry consistent with the approved model. Do not stretch the outside photograph independently of the interior or paste in a skyline at a scale that does not match the camera. If an exterior plate is used, its capture location and lens metadata should be recorded before compositing.

Give every important visual claim an evidence status.

A compact review matrix helps the architect, visualiser and sales team distinguish checked geometry from artistic decisions. It is especially useful when multiple units share similar interior materials but have different outlooks.

The status can be “verified against supplied drawings”, “modelled from approximate data”, “illustrative” or “not yet checked”. These are process descriptions, not guarantees that the built result will be identical. Keep them in the internal manifest even when the final public caption is shorter.

Write captions that say what the image actually represents.

Captions should identify the unit or typical unit, the viewpoint and the illustrative status where relevant. “Indicative view from a proposed living room; surrounding context modelled from information available on [date]” is more informative than “uninterrupted panoramic views”. Avoid “guaranteed”, “permanent”, “exact” or “actual view” unless the responsible team has evidence and has approved that precise claim.

A genuine photograph from an existing completed apartment can be called a photograph, with the capture position and date recorded. A digitally constructed view from an unbuilt apartment should not be passed off as a photograph taken from that apartment. Likewise, an interior render with a generic skyline should be identified as an atmosphere illustration rather than a view from a named unit.

Keep captions readable on mobile and in print, not hidden in tiny legal text. Have the developer's legal and sales teams review the language under the rules applicable to the market. This article is a visualisation workflow, not jurisdiction-specific advertising or property-law advice.

The useful part is the decision trail. A compelling image can remain a draft when its evidence is incomplete. Once missing context is checked, issue a new revision rather than overwriting the original and losing the reason for the change.

Keep the view study reproducible through revisions.

Deliver the approved image alongside a small record: unit and view IDs, source-model revisions, coordinate system, camera transform, eye height, field of view, context source and date, identified uncertainties, caption, reviewer and approval date. Archive the neutral geometry-check frame separately from the marketing grade. If a buyer later questions a claimed view, the team should be able to identify what was represented and on what basis.

When the building or a neighbour changes, rerun affected view checks. A balcony adjustment, floor-height revision or new tree can invalidate an earlier image even if the room layout is unchanged. Use the rendering change-control checklist to trace updates across the development website and campaign materials.

For a website with a unit selector, link each image to the correct unit ID and do not reuse a generic “premium view” rendering across apartments with materially different orientations. The interactive floor-plan guide

Release checklist for apartment-view visualisations.

  • The purpose is stated: atmosphere, indicative outlook or survey-supported study.
  • Unit ID, plan revision, facade revision and finished-floor level are confirmed.
  • Camera position is marked on the plan and its eye height and orientation are recorded.
  • Window frames, parapets, balcony slabs, screens and neighbouring project wings are modelled.
  • Context geometry has a source, date and explicit accuracy status.
  • Known nearby proposals and seasonal vegetation have been considered where material.
  • Projection, field of view and crop are documented and not used to imply a different viewpoint.
  • Neutral geometry-check frames and the final marketing treatment are reviewed separately.
  • Illustrative elements and limitations are communicated in an approved, legible caption.
  • No unverified permanent, guaranteed or unobstructed-view claims appear in the copy.
  • Image rights and the intended publication channels are cleared by the responsible parties.
  • The release manifest identifies reviewer, date, view ID and all relevant revisions.

Accurate view communication is not about making every window look less attractive. It is about letting a developer show what is genuinely compelling without quietly converting assumptions into promises. A documented eye point and an honest caption are often more valuable than another round of cinematic colour grading.
