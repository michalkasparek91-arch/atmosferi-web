How to review external sun-shading louvers in architectural renderings.

An elegant screen can conceal incorrect blade geometry, unsupported fixings or misleading shadows. This source-led review keeps the facade design traceable.

A louver is not just a texture

External sun-shading systems are easy to beautify accidentally. A thin dark stripe in a rendering may stand for a deep aluminium blade with brackets, end caps and a very different shadow. Before rendering, clarify whether the image is intended to compare facade options, explain the proposed building to planners or communicate a fixed design. The rendering can help reviewers judge appearance; it cannot certify solar performance, structural adequacy or compliance.

Build a revision-controlled source pack

Ask for the current elevation, typical facade section, louver schedule, structural fixing concept and a marked-up drawing of any motorised zones. Record the drawing issue, model version, orientation and person responsible for sign-off. Distinguish designed dimensions from assumptions. When a manufacturer has not been selected, avoid assigning a real product name or implying a tested performance rating.

Check blade pitch, depth and angle separately

Pitch is the centre-to-centre spacing; depth is how far a blade projects; tilt is its orientation. These variables produce distinct visual effects and should not be traded off to match a reference photograph. Compare a frontal view with a grazing view: narrow blades can look substantial head-on while deep fins dominate oblique views. Include end conditions and interruptions at windows and corners. A repeated pattern that ignores floor levels or structural bays may be visually tidy but architecturally false.

Horizontal versus vertical shading

Horizontal blades, vertical fins and perforated screens respond differently to facade orientation and viewpoint. Do not simply rotate a material map when the design switches systems. Check whether horizontal blades sit above glazing, across the full height or within a dedicated frame. For vertical fins, verify rotation direction, left and right corner terminations and whether adjacent fins overlap in perspective. Refer to the architect's elevation, not an aesthetic guess.

Show credible brackets and clearances

The support structure can determine whether the shading system looks light or heavy. Check offsets from the glazing, fixing points, intermediate rails, slab-edge connections and clearance at operable windows. Do not hide supports behind an impossible floating blade arrangement. Conversely, avoid inventing heavy steel members where the design has only a preliminary fixing strategy. Label unresolved connections as illustrative.

Distinguish fixed and adjustable systems

An adjustable system should not be rendered as if all blades can occupy any position without mechanical limits. Request a defined presentation state, such as fully open or a documented design angle. Record it with the image version. A closed-blade image can suggest a darker interior and more privacy than the typical operating condition. If animation is requested, require the designer to confirm the range of movement before showing it.

Use consistent solar assumptions

Shadows from deep fins are a geometry check as much as a lighting effect. Lock north, project location, date, time and time zone for comparisons. Inspect the shadow edge at glazing and reveals; missing or inconsistent self-shadowing can reveal a disconnected louver model. A single sunny image is not evidence of annual overheating reduction or daylight sufficiency. For the separate verification workflow, see the sun and shadow accuracy checklist.

Do not use dark glazing to compensate

A common visual shortcut is to darken the glass until the shading looks stronger. This changes two variables at once and makes option comparisons unreliable. Keep glazing material, interior brightness, camera and exposure fixed while reviewing louver geometry. If the glass specification also changes, present it as a separate option. The glazing reflection guide covers this second decision.

Check cleaning and maintenance visibility

External blades may affect window cleaning, access to facade joints and maintenance of operable mechanisms. A visualisation should not imply a clear access path where a deep screen blocks it. Ask whether access equipment or removable panels are designed. When unresolved, show the physical geometry accurately and record the operational question for the project team rather than erasing it from the image.

Fictional example: a west-facing office facade

Fictional illustrative example, not an Atmosferi commission. A proposed office block has vertical fins on its west elevation. The first draft uses evenly spaced flat strips, while the design drawing specifies deeper blades at a fixed angle with visible mounting rails. The team requests two locked-camera images: one with the current fin geometry and one with a revised design option. Both retain the same glazing, sun position and context. The review concerns appearance only, not energy performance.

Create an approval record that survives revisions

Export the chosen view with camera ID, source drawing revision, blade dimensions, assumed angle and lighting condition. Separate comments about facade rhythm from comments about engineering and solar performance. If a later design change alters the fin depth or spacing, identify every affected image and update the published-render change log. An attractive image without a source trail is not a reliable approval record.

External shading visual review checklist

  • Facade orientation and latest elevation are identified.
  • Blade pitch, depth, thickness and tilt are verified separately.
  • Horizontal or vertical system is modelled as designed.
  • Brackets, rails and edge terminations are credible or labelled provisional.
  • Window opening and cleaning access conflicts are flagged.
  • Fixed versus adjustable blade positions are distinguished.
  • Sun position, exposure and camera are locked for option comparisons.
  • Glazing material is not quietly changed to flatter the shading.
  • At least one oblique view tests apparent depth and spacing.
  • Unresolved specifications and responsible approvers are recorded.

Use the facade consultant's drawings and specialist studies for technical performance. A rendering is not a substitute for those documents.
